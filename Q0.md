## Physics motivating a mathematical framework


In quantum mechanics there are some familiar actors: Constituents of matter such as
atomic and subatomic particles; electrons, neutrons, protons, and photons (light particles). There are also more *exotic* particles such as antimatter, neutrinos, and muons. In the 1980s we were still historically close enough to the origins of quantum mechanics that we taught the subject as a historical progression of 
interesting events: successes and failures and *surprises*. As an example: A great many clues about the quantum world were the byproduct of a Scottish scientist's attempts to create and study clouds in a laboratory.


I mention "in a laboratory" to invoke the idea of controlled environments that gradually
lead to studying *systems*. Systems in isolation in turn get us to *degrees of freedom*.
So one quantum system might be distinct from another in its details and yet they can be 
compared in the abstract in terms of degrees of freedom. In the narrative that follows 
I eventually map the simplest non-trivial quantum computing system (a qubit) 
to the double slit experiment. This mapping may amount to sleight of hand but
I will make every effort to show in detail how the trick is done.


But before quantum mechanics can begin: For a coherent and entertaining story we need a 
mathematical stage upon which our particles and their degrees of freedom act.


The little ansatz to start off is: We have about five axioms of quantum mechanics that I
will not state just yet. But in consequence -- to support these axioms 
mathematically -- we build up a formalism called a *Hilbert space*. That is, Hilbert space 
supports quantum mechanical narratives, particularly through the features of complex
numbers. 


This first chapter is entirely mathematics with the occasional nod to physical motivation. 


## Fields


In the mathematical topic of *analysis* a set $S$ is understood to be a collection of distinct elements. Add to this set two *operations*: addition and multiplication: To be applied between any two elements of the set. $S$ is *closed* under addition and multiplication if all sums / products are also in $S$. 


Taking S to be closed under addition: Add four addition axioms where $a, \; b, \; c \; \in \; S$: 


- **Associativity**: $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$.
- **Identity element** There is $0 \in S$ such that for all $a$ in $S$ we have $a + 0 = a$.
- **Additive inverse**: For every $a$ in $S$ there is $-a$ also in $S$ such that $a + (-a) = 0$.
- **Commutation**: For every $a$ and $b$ in $S$ we have $a + b = b + a$. Together with closure all this means that $S$ is an abelian group under addition. 


These addition axioms behave as first or *preexisting* conditions. Now we try for the same axioms under the *multiplication* operation. 


- **Associativity**: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$ (ok)
- **Multiplicative identity**: There is "$1 \in S$" such that $1 \cdot a = a \cdot 1 = a$ (ok) 
- **Multiplicative inverse**: For all $a \in S$ *except for the additive identity $0$* there is an $a^{-1} \in S$ such that $a \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1} \cdot a = 1$ (ok but for the peculiar exception)


Here is the pause: The identity element for addition $0$ has no multiplicative inverse. Why? Because $0$ multiplied by anything must be $0$. This is a theorem and here is a short proof that depends on *distributivity* (as yet unstated): 


$0 \cdot a = (0 + 0) \cdot a = 0 \cdot a + 0 \cdot a$, subtract $(0 \cdot a)$ from both extremes to arrive at $0 = 0 \cdot a$. Since $a$ is any element of $S$ it follows there is no $a$ for which $0 \cdot a = 1$, that is: there is no multiplicative inverse for $0$. 


Consequence: A multiplicative inverse exists for all elements of $S$ except the additive identity $0.$ This is the *multiplicative* inverse axiom for $S \backslash \{ 0 \}$.


Fourth multiplication axiom:


- **Commutation**: $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$.


A set with closure, associativity, identity and inverses is a *group*. With the additional property of commutivity it is called an *abelian group*; so we have now defined two of these, $(S, +)$ and $(S \backslash \{0\}, \times)$.


Next an axiom connecting addition to multiplication:


- **Distributive axiom**: $a \cdot (b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c$. 


Technically we should state both left and right distributivity, but since multiplication commutes in this field they are equivalent. We have combined three axiom sets: addition-abelian, multiplication-abelian, and this distributive axiom. The result is called a **field** $\mathcal{F}$. This is the one-third point of the mathematical journey. 


A field defined in this way provides us with *elements of the field* also known as *scalars*. A *scalar field* is an important ingredient of what defines a *vector space*; which is in turn a component of defining a *Hilbert space*. And lastly (for now) the Hilbert space will support the notion of *states* which are vectors used to define quantum systems; so the transition to quantum mechanics is now a light at the end of this tunnel.


The supply of scalars in the field $\mathcal{F}$ has been defined in terms of abstract elements without specifically saying "these are real numbers" or "these are complex numbers" or what have you. In the practice of quantum mechanics we will use $\mathcal{F} = \mathbb{C}$, the complex numbers, owing to their enhancements above the real numbers.


### From scalar field to vector space


A *vector space* $\mathcal{V}$ over a field $\mathcal{F}$ is a set of elements called 'vectors' with two operations defined. As above these two operations will follow certain rules. (At this point a vector is very abstract.)


The first vector operation is *vector addition*; applying between two elements $u, \; v \; \in \;\mathcal{V}$ with closure: $u + v = w \in \mathcal{V}$. 


The second operation is *scalar multiplication*. Take a scalar $a \in \mathcal{F}$. Technically scalar multiplication is a *map*, not an *operation*, symbolically $\mathcal{F} \times \mathcal{V} \to \mathcal{V}$.


The vector space $\mathcal{V}$ satisfies:


- $(\mathcal{V}, +)$ is an abelian group: Closure, Associativity, Identity, Inverses, Commutativity
- Scalar multiplication $a \cdot v$ follows these four axioms
    - Compatibility with field multiplication: $a(bv) = (ab)v$
    - The $\mathcal{F}$ multiplicative identity $1$ operates trivially: $1 \cdot v = v$
    - Distributivity over vector addition: $a \cdot (u + v) = au + av$
    - Distributivity over field addition: $(a + b)v = av + bv$



The vector space $\mathcal{V}$ being an abelian group via vector addition echoes the field $\mathcal{F}$.
Scalar multiplication for $\mathcal{V}$ resembles multiplication in the field $\mathcal{F}$. And
Compatibility and Distributivity axioms in the vector space echo the distributive 
axiom for $\mathcal{F}$. The distinction is we define *multiplication by scalars* rather than *multiplication between elements of $\mathcal{V}$*.


In what follows we eventually define at least three types of vector - vector multiplication: Inner products, outer products and tensor products. But they are not part of the staging just yet.


### Linear independence, span, basis, dimension


Philosophically, the pragmatic pivot in defining a vector space is the
notion of a *basis* that follows directly from the axioms above.  (An additional axiom called the *axiom of choice* is also needed for infinite-dimensional vector spaces but this is a little far afield at the moment.) A basis in turn
leads to a *dimension* for a vector space, a positive integer (or $\infty$), indicating
the space's supported complexity. In linear algebra terminology, dimension is conferred by linear independence and span. A single qubit (being the simplest
quantum system) resides in a two-dimensional space. 


Defining necessary terminology: A **linear combination** of vectors is a sum of constituent vectors each with a scalar coefficient: $a_1 \cdot v_1 + a_2 \cdot v_2 + \dots + a_n \cdot v_n$.



**Linear independence:** (*'You can't get there from 'here'* principle) A set of vectors $\{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k\}$ is linearly independent 
if the only solution to 


$$a_1 v_1 + a_2 v_2 + \cdots + a_k v_k = \mathbf{0}$$


is $a_1 = a_2 = \cdots = a_k = 0$. In other words, no vector in a linearly independent 
set can be written as a combination of the others.


A little elaboration on linear independence: The $0$ in the above can be a little misdirecting. Move $a_i v_i$ to the right side as $-a_i v_i$ to see this vector expressed as a linear combination of the others. *Linear independence* means there is no non-trivial way to do this: You can't get to $v_i$ using a sum of the other vectors. ('Trivial' means *with all zero coefficients*.) 


**Span:** The span of a set of vectors is the collection of all linear combinations of those 
vectors. If $\text{span}(\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\}) = \mathcal{V}$, the set *spans* $\mathcal{V}$. You can reach 
every vector in $\mathcal{V}$ by combining the $k$ vectors in that set with scalar coefficients.
(*'You can get there from here'* principle.)


With linear independence and span defined we now define a basis. The basis walks the fine line between *you can't get there from here* and *you can get there from here* to arrive at *there is only one way to get there from here*.


**Basis:** A set of vectors that is both linearly independent *and* spans $\mathcal{V}$ is a 
*basis* for $\mathcal{V}$. Every vector in $\mathcal{V}$ can be written *uniquely* as a 
linear combination of basis vectors: Span provides *at least* one construction
and linear independence provides *at most one* construction. The scalar coefficients 
of the basis vectors in a given linear combination are called *coordinates*.
From linear independence: the $0$ vector coordinates are all zero. 


Uniqueness in decomposing an arbitrary vector $v$ makes coordinates well-defined.


**Dimension** is defined as the number of vectors in any basis of $\mathcal{V}$. There is a theorem that guarantees 
all bases of a given vector space have the same size, so dimension is well-defined. If $\mathcal{V}$ 
has a finite basis with $n$ elements, $\dim(\mathcal{V}) = n$. If no finite basis exists, $\mathcal{V}$ is infinite-dimensional.


Suppose a basis exists as elements of $\mathcal{V}$: $e_1, e_2, \dots, e_n$. We can write such a 
basis as $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$. Then any vector $v \in \mathcal{V}$ can be written in terms of unique scalar coordinates $c_i$ as:


$$v = c_1 e_1 + c_2 e_2 + \cdots + c_n e_n$$


Again the scalars $c_i \in \mathcal{F}$ are **components** or **coordinates** of $v$ in that basis. This is the 'materialization' of a vector as an ordered set of scalar coordinates through the unique linear combination formalism. When we write a vector as a sequence of coordinates we are (often invisibly) invoking an underlying basis. But this basis is not necessarily unique; so if we change the basis, that same vector $v$ will be expressed as a different set of coordinates. The vector as an abstract element of the vector space is basis-independent, while its representation is not.


A vector space can be finite or infinite-dimensional. To give one concrete example 
in the finite case: For a two-dimensional vector space with basis $\{e_1, e_2\}$, 
a vector can be written as a column:


$$e_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = [1, 0]^T, \quad e_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} = [0, 1]^T$$


Any vector in this space is then $v = c_1 e_1 + c_2 e_2 = [c_1, c_2]^T$.


Notice that this is a column representation of the basis vectors and $v$.


## From vector space to Hilbert space


We have built up from sets through fields to a vector space, arriving at concepts of basis (from *span* and *linear independence*) and dimension. To reach Hilbert space we introduce multiplication defined for two vectors $u, \; v \in \mathcal{V}$: The *inner product*. With the inner product comes a *norm* and *completeness*. 


### Definition


A **Hilbert space** $\mathcal{H}$ is a vector space equipped with an inner product that is also complete with respect to the norm induced by that inner product.


### 1. Start with a Vector Space


Begin with a vector space $\mathcal{V}$ over the field $\mathcal{F}$. We have two such fields at hand, namely  $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$. Switching to practicality for a moment: $\mathbb{C}$ will prove to have flexibility not available in $\mathbb{R}$ and this will be necessary for quantum mechanics.


### 2. Define an Inner Product


An inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : \mathcal{V} \times \mathcal{V} \to \mathcal{F}$ must satisfy:


- **Conjugate symmetry:** $\langle x, y \rangle = \overline{\langle y, x \rangle}$
- **Linearity in the second argument:** $\langle x, \alpha y + \beta z \rangle = \alpha \langle x, y \rangle + \beta \langle x, z \rangle$
- **Positive definiteness:** $\langle x, x \rangle \geq 0$ with equality iff $x = 0$



### 3. Induce a Norm


The inner product induces a norm (the square root of the self-inner product):


$$\|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x, x \rangle}$$


An inner product operation produces a scalar from the field $\mathcal{F}$ which as indicated above 
will be $\mathbb{C}$. So in general an inner product in Hilbert space produces a complex number. 
The norm is defined as the (by convention positive) square root of the inner product of $x$ with
itself. This self-inner-product must adhere to positive definiteness meaning it evaluates to 
a positive real number (or zero when $x$ is the zero vector). This arrangement for the norm 
also agrees with conjugate symmetry.


As a *mathematical foundations* note: The norm satisfies the **parallelogram law** 
$\|x + y\|^2 + \|x - y\|^2 = 2\|x\|^2 + 2\|y\|^2$: The geometry of Hilbert space is "round" (Euclidean), not "diamond-shaped" or "square." It guarantees that the unit ball is an ellipse (or its higher-dimensional analog), never a diamond or a square. This roundness is what makes projection, orthogonal decomposition, and the spectral theorem work. (Important elements of quantum mechanics.)



### 4. Complete the Space


A Cauchy sequence $(x_n)$ is one where the terms get arbitrarily close to one another.
Formally: For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that for all $m, n > N$, $\lVert x_m - x_n \rVert < \varepsilon$. Notice this is about closeness of vectors in the sequence; not related to some fixed point.


A space is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence converges within the space. 


If the inner 
product space is not already complete, we have a technical gap. There is a ready solution, "take the completion of the inner product space", but this is starting to get a little far afield into the subject matter. Here we read out the recipe for bridging this final gap:


- Identify all Cauchy sequences in $\mathcal{V}$
- Define equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences (two sequences are equivalent if their difference converges to zero)
- The set of equivalence classes, with the extended inner product, forms the Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$


And now to summarize:

- We have a field $\mathcal{F}$.
- We have a vector space $\mathcal{V}$ that depends upon $\mathcal{F}$.
- We have the notion of *basis* and *dimensionality*.
- We define an *inner product* from this vector space and from this pick up the *norm*.
- We require completeness of the inner product space...
- ...and this is how we define Hilbert space, the mathematical framework for quantum mechanics, particularly the matrix formalism.
