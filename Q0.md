## Vector fields to Hilbert space


In quantum mechanics there are known to be some familiar actors, constituents of matter, 
the subatomic particles: electrons, positrons, neutrons, protons, photons, helium nuclei 
($\alpha$ particles), muons; and so on. There are as well more exotic particles such as
neutrinos and dark matter and who knows what all. For these particles to provide us 
with a coherent and hopefully entertaining story we need a mathematical stage. So I begin my narrative with a 
little ansatz, an unsupported assertion: We must first develop the mathematical formalism
of *Hilbert space* upon which we will build our quantum mechanical narratives. 
The first part that follows is almost entirely mathematics. The second

So without further ado...


### Fields


In the mathematical topic of *analysis* a set $S$ is understood to be a collection of distinct elements. Add to this set two *operations*: addition and multiplication: To be applied between any two elements of the set. $S$ is *closed* under addition and multiplication if all sums / products are also in $S$. 


Taking such an S to be closed under addition: Add four axioms. First: for all $a$, $b$, $c$ in $S$ we have $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$: **associativity**. Second there is an **identity element** called $0$ such that for all $a$ in $S$ we have $a + 0 = a$. Third we bring in **additive inverses**: For every $a$ in $S$ there is $-a$ also in $S$ such that $a + (-a) = 0$. Fourth addition is **commutative**: For every $a$ and $b$ in $S$ we have $a + b = b + a$. Together with closure all this means that $S$ is an abelian group under addition. 


Now we try for the same story for our second operation *multiplication*. 
Associativity first: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$. Next a multiplicative identity we
call "$1$" (in S). The third axiom hits a snag: We want multiplicative inverses for all $a$ in 
$S$ but the $0$ element we mentioned above, the identity element for addition, has no multiplicative
 inverse. Why? Because $0$ multiplied by anything must be $0$. This is a theorem so in the spirit of 
 a thorough narrative let us see this as a proof: 


$0 \cdot a = (0 + 0) \cdot a = 0 \cdot a + 0 \cdot a$, subtract $0 \cdot a$ from both sides, get $0 = 0 \cdot a$. 


A proper inverse of $0$ such that $0 \cdot 0^{-1} = 1$ is nowhere to be found. So we refine the 
second 'multiplication' abelian group by stipulating all elements of $S$ stay but for $0$.
Now for $S \backslash \{ 0 \}$ we have a multiplicative inverse axiom. 
And fourth axium: Multiplication commutes in abelian groups.


So with a little finagling: 
We have abelian groups defined for $(S, +)$ and for $(S \backslash \{0\}, \times)$. Finally one more axiom is needed that relates addition and multiplication, the distributive axiom: $a(b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c$. (Technically we should state both left and right distributivity, but since multiplication commutes in this
field, they are equivalent.) This combination of +-abelian, x-abelian and the distributive axiom 
brings us to a **field** $F$. 


We have defined a field in this way to provide *scalars*: Elements of a field. 
This is a prerequisite to defining a *vector space*.
In turn a prerequisite to defining a *Hilbert space*. 
Which in turn will support *states*, vectors used to define quantum systems.


### The vector space


A *vector space* $V$ over a field $F$ is a set of elements called 'vectors' that has two operations defined; where these two operations follow certain rules. The first operation is vector addition; so it applies between two elements of $V$, as in  $u + v$. The second operation is 'scalar multiplication' for some scalar $a \in F$. (Technically scalar multiplication is a *map* rather than an *operation*, symbolically $F \times V \to V$.) 


The vector space $V$ must satisfy these rules:


- $(V, +)$ is an abelian group: Associativity, Identity, Inverses, Commutativity
- Scalar multiplication $a \cdot v$ follows these four axioms
    - Compatibility with field multiplication: $a(bv) = (ab)v$
    - The field $F$'s multiplicative identity $1$ operates trivially: $1 \cdot v = v$
    - Distributivity over vector addition: $a \cdot (u + v) = au + av$
    - Distributivity over field addition: $(a + b)v = av + bv$


So $V$ defined as such is resonating with the definition of the field.
The vector space being an abelian group via vector addition echoes the field $F$.
Scalar multiplication for $V$ echoes multiplication in the field $F$. And
Compatibility and Distributivity axioms in the vector space echo the distributive 
axiom for $F$. The shift is *multiplication by scalars* rather multiplication 
between elements of $V$.  


### Linear independence, span, basis, dimension


**Linear independence:** (You can't get there from here principle) 
A set of vectors $\{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k\}$ is linearly independent 
if the only solution to 


$$a_1 v_1 + a_2 v_2 + \cdots + a_k v_k = \mathbf{0}$$


is $a_1 = a_2 = \cdots = a_k = 0$. In other words, no vector in a linearly independent 
set can be written as a combination of the others.


**Span:** The span of a set of vectors is the collection of all linear combinations of those 
vectors. If $\text{span}(\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\}) = V$, the set *spans* $V$ — you can reach 
every vector in $V$ by combining the $k$ vectors in that set with scalar coefficients.
(You can get there from here principle)


With linear independence and span defined we now define a basis.


**Basis:** A set of vectors that is both linearly independent *and* spans $V$ is a 
*basis* for $V$. The crucial property: every vector in $V$ can be written *uniquely* as a 
linear combination of basis vectors and this now includes the $0$ vector. Uniqueness (in
decomposition) is what makes coordinates (components) well-defined.


**Dimension:** The number of vectors in any basis of $V$. A foundational theorem guarantees 
all bases of a given vector space have the same size, so dimension is well-defined. If $V$ 
has a finite basis with $n$ elements, $\dim(V) = n$. If no finite basis exists, $V$ is 
infinite-dimensional.


With a basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ in hand, any vector $v \in V$ can be written as:


$$v = c_1 e_1 + c_2 e_2 + \cdots + c_n e_n$$


where the scalars $c_i \in F$ are the **components** (or coordinates) of $v$ in that basis. 
Change the basis and the same abstract vector gets different components. The vector is 
basis-independent; its representation is not.


A vector space can be finite or infinite-dimensional. To give one concrete example 
in the finite case: For a two-dimensional vector space with basis $\{e_1, e_2\}$, 
a vector can be written as a column:


$$e_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = [1, 0]^T, \quad e_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} = [0, 1]^T$$


Any vector in this space is then $v = c_1 e_1 + c_2 e_2 = [c_1, c_2]^T$.


## From vector space to Hilbert space


So far we have built up from sets through fields to defining a vector space, arriving just now at the basis (from *span* and *linear independence*) and dimension. The remaining piece needed to reach a Hilbert space is a second type of multiplication defined for two vectors $u, \; v \in V$: The *inner product*. With the inner product we also have a norm, and we have completeness.


### Definition


A **Hilbert space** $\mathcal{H}$ is a vector space equipped with an inner product that is also complete with respect to the norm induced by that inner product.


### 1. Start with a Vector Space


Begin with a vector space $V$ over the field $\mathbb{F}$. We have two such fields at hand, namely  $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ where $\mathbb{C}$ has the flexibility needed for
quantum mechanics.


### 2. Define an Inner Product


An inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : V \times V \to \mathbb{F}$ must satisfy:


- **Conjugate symmetry:** $\langle x, y \rangle = \overline{\langle y, x \rangle}$
- **Linearity in one argument:** $\langle x, \alpha y + \beta z \rangle = \alpha \langle x, y \rangle + \beta \langle x, z \rangle$
- **Positive definiteness:** $\langle x, x \rangle \geq 0$ with equality iff $x = 0$



### 3. Induce a Norm


The inner product induces a norm:


$$\|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x, x \rangle}$$


An inner product operation produces a scalar from the field $\mathbb{F}$ which I indicated above 
will be $\mathbb{C}$. So in general an inner product in Hilbert space produces a complex number. 
The norm is defined as the (by convention positive) square root of the inner product of $x$ with
itself. This self-inner-product must adhere to positive definiteness meaning it evaluates to 
a positive real number (or zero when $x$ is the zero vector). This arrangement for the norm 
also agrees with conjugate symmetry.


As a *mathematical foundations* note: The norm satisfies the **parallelogram law** 
$\|x + y\|^2 + \|x - y\|^2 = 2\|x\|^2 + 2\|y\|^2$
which in turn underpins physical tools used in quantum mechanics.
The parallelogram law says: the geometry of Hilbert space is "round" (Euclidean), 
not "diamond-shaped" or "square." It guarantees that the unit ball is an ellipse 
(or its higher-dimensional analog), never a diamond or a square. This roundness 
is what makes projection, orthogonal decomposition, and the spectral theorem 
work — all of which quantum mechanics relies on.



### 4. Complete the Space


A space is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence converges within the space. If the inner 
product space is not already complete, we take its completion:


- Identify all Cauchy sequences in $V$
- Define equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences (two sequences are equivalent if their difference converges to zero)
- The set of equivalence classes, with the extended inner product, forms the Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$


### Example: an inner product space that is not complete

Let $V$ be the set of all continuous functions $f: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$, with inner 
product:

$$\langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^1 f(x) \, g(x) \, dx$$

This satisfies all the inner product axioms (linearity, symmetry, positive-definiteness). 
It is a perfectly valid inner product space — but it is **not** complete.

Consider the sequence of continuous functions:

$$f_n(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } x \in [0, \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{n}] \\ \text{linear ramp} & \text{if } x \in [\tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{n}, \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{n}] \\ 1 & \text{if } x \in [\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{n}, 1] \end{cases}$$

Each $f_n$ is continuous (the ramp connects 0 to 1 smoothly). As $n \to \infty$, the ramp 
gets steeper and the functions "try to converge" to the step function:

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x < \tfrac{1}{2} \\ 1 & x > \tfrac{1}{2} \end{cases}$$

This is a Cauchy sequence in the $L^2$ norm ($\|f_n - f_m\| \to 0$ as $n, m \to \infty$), 
but the limit $f$ is **not continuous** — it has a jump at $x = 1/2$. The limit does not 
live in $V$. The sequence converges to something *outside the space*. There is a "hole."

The fix: enlarge the space to $L^2([0,1])$ — the space of all square-integrable functions 
(not just continuous ones). Now step functions are included, the Cauchy sequence has its 
limit, and the space is complete. $L^2([0,1])$ with this inner product is a Hilbert space.

The analogy is: continuous functions on [0,1] are to $L^2$ as $\mathbb{Q}$ is to 
$\mathbb{R}$. Completion fills the holes.


### Examples

| Space | Inner Product | Notes |
|-------|--------------|-------|
| $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $\langle x, y \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i$ | Finite-dimensional, automatically complete |
| $\ell^2$ | $\langle x, y \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^\infty x_i \overline{y_i}$ | Space of square-summable sequences |
| $L^2([a,b])$ | $\langle f, g \rangle = \int_a^b f(t)\overline{g(t)}\, dt$ | Completion of continuous functions |


### Important Properties

1. **Riesz Representation Theorem:** Every bounded linear functional on $\mathcal{H}$ can be represented as an inner product with a fixed element.

2. **Orthonormal Bases:** Every Hilbert space admits an orthonormal basis $\{e_i\}$ such that any $x \in \mathcal{H}$ can be written as:
$$x = \sum_i \langle x, e_i \rangle \, e_i$$

3. **Projection Theorem:** For any closed subspace $M \subseteq \mathcal{H}$, every element $x$ has a unique decomposition:
$$x = x_M + x_{M^\perp}, \quad x_M \in M, \quad x_{M^\perp} \in M^\perp$$


### Why Completeness Matters

Without completeness, we lose:
- Convergence guarantees for orthogonal expansions
- The projection theorem (closest point in a closed subspace)
- The spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators
- The foundation for quantum mechanics, where states live in $\mathcal{H}$
