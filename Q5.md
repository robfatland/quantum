# Q5: Bases in $\mathbb{C}^2$ — the Y-basis and the two-plane picture

## Setup: the Z-basis in $\mathbb{C}^2$

We work directly in $\mathbb{C}^2$ rather than on the Bloch sphere. A state 
$|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ is described by two complex numbers, 
which we visualize as points on two complex unit disks:

- **$C_0$ (the $\alpha$ plane):** point at radius $r_\alpha$ and angle $\eta$
- **$C_1$ (the $\beta$ plane):** point at radius $r_\beta$ and angle $\nu$

Constraint: $r_\alpha^2 + r_\beta^2 = 1$.

For the Z-basis vectors:

**$|0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$:**
- $C_0$: $r_\alpha = 1$, $\eta = 0$ (at the rightmost point of the unit circle)
- $C_1$: $r_\beta = 0$, $\nu$ undefined (at the origin)

**$|1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$:**
- $C_0$: $r_\alpha = 0$, $\eta$ undefined (at the origin)
- $C_1$: $r_\beta = 1$, $\nu = 0$ (at the rightmost point of the unit circle)


## Motivating eigenvalues from transformations in $\mathbb{C}^2$


### The question

We want to characterize the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the three Pauli operators 
($\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y$, $\sigma_z$). These correspond to π-rotations in the single-qubit 
Hilbert space. But π-rotations are defined on the Bloch sphere, and we want to stay in 
$\mathbb{C}^2$. So the first task is: what does a π-rotation *mean* in the two-plane 
picture?


### What $\sigma_z$ does in $\mathbb{C}^2$

The matrix $\sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\0 & -1\end{pmatrix}$ acts on a state as:

$$\sigma_z \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\-\beta\end{pmatrix}$$

In the two-plane picture:

- **$C_0$ ($\alpha$ plane):** $\alpha \to \alpha$ — nothing changes. Same $r_\alpha$, same $\eta$.
- **$C_1$ ($\beta$ plane):** $\beta \to -\beta$ — the point flips through the origin. Same $r_\beta$, but $\nu \to \nu + \pi$.

This is specific: $\sigma_z$ rotates *only* $C_1$ by π. It does not touch $C_0$. It is 
not a choice of "either one" — the matrix dictates which component gets the sign flip.

**Initial (incorrect) intuition:** "a π-rotation for $\sigma_z$ corresponds to no change 
in $r_\alpha/r_\beta$ but either $\eta$ or $\nu$ changes by π." This is almost right but 
imprecise — it's specifically $\nu$ (the $\beta$ phase) that shifts, not a free choice.

**What about global phase?** Yes, one could always add a global phase $\gamma$ to both 
$\eta$ and $\nu$ without changing physics. But that's not part of what $\sigma_z$ does — 
that's a separate degree of freedom that exists before and after. The action of $\sigma_z$ 
is unambiguous: leave $\alpha$ alone, negate $\beta$.

**Effect on the relative phase:** Before $\sigma_z$: relative phase is 
$\phi = \nu - \eta$. After: $\phi' = (\nu + \pi) - \eta = \phi + \pi$. The relative 
phase advances by π — which is exactly the 180° rotation about z on the Bloch sphere. 
The two pictures agree.


### Eigenvalues of $\sigma_z$: the fixed points

An eigenvector of an operator is a state that the operator leaves unchanged *up to a 
scalar multiple* (the eigenvalue). In $\mathbb{C}^2$:

**$|0\rangle$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue +1:**

$$\sigma_z|0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\0 & -1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = (+1)|0\rangle$$

In the two-plane picture: $C_1$ gets rotated by π, but $r_\beta = 0$ — the point is at 
the origin of the $C_1$ disk, so rotating it does nothing. The state is entirely unchanged. 
Eigenvalue +1 means "this state is a fixed point of the operator."

**$|1\rangle$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $-1$:**

$$\sigma_z|1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\0 & -1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\-1\end{pmatrix} = (-1)|1\rangle$$

In the two-plane picture: $C_0$ has $r_\alpha = 0$ (untouched anyway). $C_1$ flips 
$\beta = 1$ to $\beta = -1$ (i.e. $\nu$ goes from 0 to π). But $-|1\rangle$ and 
$|1\rangle$ differ only by global phase (specifically $e^{i\pi} = -1$) — they represent 
the same physical state (same ray in Hilbert space).

**The subtlety:** "Eigenvalue +1 means nothing changes" is clear. "Eigenvalue $-1$ means 
the vector picks up a minus sign yet it's still the same ray" can feel contradictory. The 
resolution: eigenvalues are algebraic facts about the linear map, not physical statements 
about distinguishable states. The eigenvalue $\pm 1$ is what you would *measure* if 
$\sigma_z$ were used as an observable. The eigenvector tells you which state gives that 
definite measurement outcome.


### The plan: from transform to eigenvectors to matrix

The program for each Pauli operator:

1. **Define the transformation** in $\mathbb{C}^2$ (what does it do to $\alpha$ and $\beta$?)
2. **Find eigenvectors** — which states are unchanged up to a scalar?
3. **Identify eigenvalues** — what scalar multiplies the eigenvector?
4. **Write the matrix** in the computational basis

For $\sigma_z$ this is now complete. The eigenvectors are $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ 
(the Z-basis itself) with eigenvalues +1 and $-1$. The matrix is diagonal in its own 
eigenbasis — which is why $\sigma_z$ is so simple: the computational basis *is* its 
eigenbasis.

Next: apply the same program to $\sigma_x$ (whose eigenvectors are $|+\rangle$ and 
$|-\rangle$) and then $\sigma_y$ (whose eigenvectors are $|{+i}\rangle$ and $|{-i}\rangle$).


## $\sigma_x$: what it does and its eigenvectors


### Action of $\sigma_x$ in $\mathbb{C}^2$

$$\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$$

$$\sigma_x \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\beta\\\alpha\end{pmatrix}$$

It swaps the two components. In the two-plane picture:

- The point that was on $C_0$ (at radius $r_\alpha$, angle $\eta$) moves to $C_1$
- The point that was on $C_1$ (at radius $r_\beta$, angle $\nu$) moves to $C_0$

The two disks exchange their contents. Radii swap, phases swap. No rotation within 
either disk — just a swap between them.

**Effect on the relative phase:** Before: $\phi = \nu - \eta$. After: the new $\alpha$ 
is the old $\beta$ (phase $\nu$) and the new $\beta$ is the old $\alpha$ (phase $\eta$). 
So $\phi' = \eta - \nu = -\phi$. The relative phase gets negated.


### The X-basis eigenvectors as column vectors

$$|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}, \quad |-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}$$

In the two-plane picture:

**$|+\rangle$:**
- $C_0$: $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{2}$, so $r_\alpha = 1/\sqrt{2}$, $\eta = 0$
- $C_1$: $\beta = 1/\sqrt{2}$, so $r_\beta = 1/\sqrt{2}$, $\nu = 0$
- Both disks have equal radius, same phase. Relative phase $\phi = 0$.

**$|-\rangle$:**
- $C_0$: $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{2}$, so $r_\alpha = 1/\sqrt{2}$, $\eta = 0$
- $C_1$: $\beta = -1/\sqrt{2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e^{i\pi}$, so $r_\beta = 1/\sqrt{2}$, $\nu = \pi$
- Both disks have equal radius, but the $C_1$ point is on the opposite side. 
  Relative phase $\phi = \pi$.


### Verifying they are eigenvectors

$$\sigma_x|+\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = (+1)|+\rangle$$

Swapping two identical components gives back the same vector. Eigenvalue +1.

$$\sigma_x|-\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\end{pmatrix} = (-1)|-\rangle$$

Swapping gives $(-1, 1)^T/\sqrt{2}$, which is $-1$ times the original. Eigenvalue $-1$.

**The pattern in the two-plane picture:** $\sigma_x$ swaps the disks. If both disks hold 
the same point ($|+\rangle$), swapping changes nothing — eigenvalue +1. If they hold 
opposite points ($|-\rangle$: same radius but phase differs by π), swapping negates the 
vector — eigenvalue $-1$.


### Cross-check with Bloch sphere

$\sigma_x$ swaps the two disks $C_0$ and $C_1$; on the Bloch sphere this is a 
π-rotation about the x-axis.

For $|+\rangle$ (eigenvalue +1) to be a fixed point of a rotation about x, it must lie 
*on* the x-axis — specifically at positive x. Check: $r_\alpha = r_\beta = 1/\sqrt{2}$ 
puts us on the Bloch equator ($\theta = \pi/2$), and $\phi = 0$ puts us on the positive 
x-axis. Confirmed.

For $|-\rangle$ (eigenvalue $-1$) it must lie on the negative x-axis. Check: same 
equatorial radius $1/\sqrt{2}$, but $\phi = \pi$ → negative x-axis. Confirmed.


## $\sigma_y$: what it does and its eigenvectors


### Action of $\sigma_y$ in $\mathbb{C}^2$

$$\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -i\\i & 0\end{pmatrix}$$

$$\sigma_y \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-i\beta\\i\alpha\end{pmatrix}$$

This is a swap (like $\sigma_x$) *plus* a quarter-turn in each disk:

- $C_0$ receives what was on $C_1$, but multiplied by $-i$. Multiplication by $-i$ 
  rotates a complex number by $-\pi/2$ (clockwise 90°) on the disk.
- $C_1$ receives what was on $C_0$, but multiplied by $+i$. Multiplication by $+i$ 
  rotates by $+\pi/2$ (counterclockwise 90°).

So: swap the disks, then rotate $C_0$ clockwise by 90° and $C_1$ counterclockwise 
by 90°.


### The Y-basis eigenvectors as column vectors

$$|{+i}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix}, \quad |{-i}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix}$$

In the two-plane picture:

**$|{+i}\rangle$:**
- $C_0$: $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{2}$, so $r_\alpha = 1/\sqrt{2}$, $\eta = 0$
- $C_1$: $\beta = i/\sqrt{2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e^{i\pi/2}$, so $r_\beta = 1/\sqrt{2}$, $\nu = \pi/2$
- Equal radii. Relative phase $\phi = \nu - \eta = \pi/2$.
- On the Bloch sphere: equator ($\theta = \pi/2$), azimuth $\phi = \pi/2$ → positive y-axis.

**$|{-i}\rangle$:**
- $C_0$: $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{2}$, so $r_\alpha = 1/\sqrt{2}$, $\eta = 0$
- $C_1$: $\beta = -i/\sqrt{2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-i\pi/2}$, so $r_\beta = 1/\sqrt{2}$, $\nu = -\pi/2$ (equivalently $3\pi/2$)
- Equal radii. Relative phase $\phi = -\pi/2$ (or $3\pi/2$).
- On the Bloch sphere: equator, azimuth $\phi = -\pi/2$ → negative y-axis.


### Verifying they are eigenvectors

$$\sigma_y|{+i}\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-i \cdot i\\i \cdot 1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix} = (+1)|{+i}\rangle$$

(Using $-i \cdot i = -i^2 = 1$.) Eigenvalue +1.

$$\sigma_y|{-i}\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-i \cdot (-i)\\i \cdot 1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-1\\i\end{pmatrix} = (-1)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix} = (-1)|{-i}\rangle$$

(Using $-i \cdot (-i) = i^2 = -1$.) Eigenvalue $-1$.


### The two-plane picture for $\sigma_y$ eigenstates

The pattern is the same as for $\sigma_x$ but rotated by 90°: the eigenvectors are 
states where the swap-plus-quarter-turn leaves the disk contents unchanged (eigenvalue +1) 
or negated (eigenvalue $-1$).

For $|{+i}\rangle$: the $C_1$ point is already at angle $\pi/2$ relative to $C_0$. When 
$\sigma_y$ swaps and applies the ±90° rotations, this specific phase relationship is 
preserved — a fixed point.

For $|{-i}\rangle$: the $C_1$ point is at angle $-\pi/2$ relative to $C_0$. After the 
swap-and-rotate, the vector picks up a global minus sign — eigenvalue $-1$.


### Cross-check with Bloch sphere

$\sigma_y$ is a π-rotation about the Bloch y-axis.

$|{+i}\rangle$ sits on the positive y-axis (eigenvalue +1, fixed point of y-rotation). 
$|{-i}\rangle$ sits on the negative y-axis (eigenvalue $-1$, negated by y-rotation). 
Confirmed.


## Summary: three operators, three bases

| Operator | Action in $\mathbb{C}^2$ | Eigenvectors | Eigenvalues | Bloch axis |
|----------|--------------------------|--------------|-------------|------------|
| $\sigma_z$ | Negate $\beta$ | $\|0\rangle$, $\|1\rangle$ | +1, $-1$ | z |
| $\sigma_x$ | Swap $\alpha \leftrightarrow \beta$ | $\|+\rangle$, $\|-\rangle$ | +1, $-1$ | x |
| $\sigma_y$ | Swap with $\pm i$ rotation | $\|{+i}\rangle$, $\|{-i}\rangle$ | +1, $-1$ | y |

Each Pauli operator is diagonal in its own eigenbasis and has eigenvalues $\pm 1$. The 
three eigenbases partition the Bloch equator into three mutually complementary 
measurement axes separated by 90° of azimuth ($\phi = 0, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2$) plus the 
poles.
