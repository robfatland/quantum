## The *state*


### A digression on the idea of a *quantum system*


Hilbert space is assumed to be over the complex scalar field $\mathbb{C}$. 
It has elements called *vectors*; it has the notion of *linearly independent* 
vectors; it has the notion of *spanning* the vector space; and these two
notions together give us a *basis* and a *dimension*. The dimension of this
simplest non-trivial Hilbert space is $2$. Why?  



The simplest (non-trivial) quantum system is studied as a two-dimensional 
Hilbert space; and now I have used jargon without defining it. So I define
*quantum system* not once but twice: In my language and then again in AI language:


A quantum system is an assembly of elements (particles, say) that we
study in isolation: That is, nothing else is present in the study to exert
outside influence. A hermetic little universe, so to speak. 


The AI would say: A quantum system is a set of degrees of freedom, isolated from
everything else, with a state representable as a unit vector in Hilbert space, 
whose evolution is unitary. 


The AI sounds like it is just being more complicated; but it is actually 
distinguishing a quantum system from a classical system by means of the word *state*.
It is saying that the quantum system has a state found within a state space -- the
Hilbert space -- that is represented as a *vector*. This in contrast to a classical
system whose state is represented as a *point*. The distinction is not vector versus
point as such -- both are ordered sets of numbers -- but rather how they are 
manipulated to make measurements. Quantum mechanics has 'more machinery' -- the
concepts and rules of Hilbert space -- with no classical analog. This machinery 
enables us to describe the peculiar quantum phenomena revealed through decades of 
experiments such as the photoelectric effect, the Stern-Gerlach experiment and 
the double-slit experiment.


But one dangling bit of jargon: What does the AI saying the system has 
*unitary evolution*? (The AI likes to keep dragging us into the mathematics.)
Let us be stubbornly anti-mathematical for the moment and say that 
unitary evolution connected to the idea that quantum interference leads to 
probabilistic behavior; and unitary evolution preserves the notion that 
probability is conserved: The probabilities of various outcomes at all
times sum to $1$. *Something's gotta happen*!


### The simplest non-trivial quantum system


Operating in a non-trivial 2-dimensional Hilbert space means there are two
basis vectors that we shall label $|0\rangle$ and $1\rangle$. These have
unit length and are represented as column vectors $[1, 0]^T$ and $[0, 1]^T$.


Any vector $|a\rangle$ in $\mathcal{H}$ can be uniquely expressed in terms of 
two complex scalar coefficients $\alpha = r_\alpha \cdot e^{i \eta}$ and 
$\beta = r_\beta \cdot e^{i \nu}$ that multiply the two basis vectors.


$$|a\rangle = \alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle$$


A Hilbert space vector can consequently be written $|a\rangle = [\alpha, \beta]^T$. 


This vector is called a *state vector* or just a *state*. It describes
a quantum bit or *qubit*. This will prove to be a fairly profound concept
so we take a moment to examine a classical (ordinary) computing bit
as a point of departure into quantum computing.



A classical computing bit takes on one of two
real values, $0$ or $1$. It can be set directly by throwing a switch, it can be
set as the result of a computation, but however it is set: A classical bit is 
understood to have a definitive value, $0$ or $1$. Its state, $0$ or $1$,
is taken to persist for any amount of time regardless of how often its 
valued is read out or checked or *measured*. It is a coin on a table top
showing either 'heads' or 'tails'.



In quantum mechanics the idea of state is distinct from the idea of measurement.
The state of a qubit can change with time but it is not until the qubit is measured 
that its value is resolved. This measurement involves the inner product of the
state vector with a basis vector. 


### A first look at the inner product of two vectors


**Q:** If a quantum system is described in terms of a state vector from Hilbert
space: What does it mean to take an inner product of this vector with another 
vector? 


**A:** We want to avoid confusing "the system is in one state at a time" (which is true) 
and "the Hilbert space contains many state representations that can be invoked in an inner 
product". Suppose a qubit is in state $|a\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$: 
This is one system in one state. But the inner product, written $\langle a | b \rangle$, 
is asking the hypothetical question "What is the overlap between state 
$|a\rangle$ and state $|b\rangle$?" 


As an example: Suppose a qubit is in state $|\psi\rangle$ and we ask "What is the probability 
the qubit is measured to be in state $|\phi\rangle$?" The answer is the square of the
magnitude of the inner product of $|\psi\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle$, written as
$|\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2$ (the 'Born rule'). This inner product compares a single
system in a particular state to a possible measurement outcome expressed as another state 
vector in the same space.


Another example, orthogonality: $\langle 0 | 1 \rangle = 0$ tells us that $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are perfectly distinguishable. If the system is in $|0\rangle$, the probability of finding it in $|1\rangle$ is zero.


Another example, basis expansion: Writing $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ uses the 
inner product implicitly: $\alpha = \langle 0 | \psi \rangle$ and $\beta = \langle 1 | \psi \rangle$. 
The coefficients are inner products.


The inner product is between two vectors in Hilbert space.  One might be the current state of the system; and the other might be a basis vector, a measurement outcome, a state to compare with, or the state at a different time. 


The Hilbert space is the space of all possible states. The system occupies one state at a time. The 
geometry of the full space (angles, overlaps, orthogonality and so on) gives quantum mechanics its 
structure.


### A first visualization of $\mathbb{C}^2$


Since $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are complex numbers we can visualize them as two separate points on
two separate complex planes. Their respective magnitudes will necessarily be shown to be less
than or equal to $1$ so they are points on their respective complex unit discs. 


The ensuing narrative can become confusing; so to try and mitigate this it is helpful
to bear in mind these basic points of emphasis: 

- $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are *not* vectors; but are rather complex valued 
scalar coefficients of basis vectors $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$.
- $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are points on two distinct complex unit discs.
- As such $\alpha$ and $\beta$ contain four degrees of freedom; but two of these
will be curtailed in what follows so that practically they will yield two degrees of freedom.


## Dirac notation and the inner product


The Hilbert space inner product uses mathematical notation $\langle a, b \rangle$.
This corresponds to Dirac's "bra-ket" notation $\langle a | b \rangle$, standard in 
physics. *Inner product* denotes a map from two vectors to a complex 
number, satisfying conjugate symmetry, linearity in the second argument, and positive
definiteness.


- **Conjugate symmetry:** $\langle a | b \rangle = \overline{\langle b | a \rangle}$
- **Linearity in the second argument:** $\langle a | (\alpha b + \beta c) \rangle = \alpha\langle a | b \rangle + \beta\langle a | c \rangle$
- **Positive definiteness:** $\langle a | a \rangle > 0$ for $|a\rangle \neq 0$


For two state vectors $a$ and $b$ both on $\mathbb{C}^2$, the inner product (the *Hermitian inner product*) 
is defined: With $|a\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}a_0\\a_1\end{pmatrix}$ and 
$|b\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}b_0\\b_1\end{pmatrix}$ we have:


$$\langle a | b \rangle = a^*_0 \cdot b_0 + a^*_1 \cdot b_1$$


Dirac's notation splits a vector inner product into a "ket" $|b\rangle$ 
(column vector) and a "bra" $\langle a|$ (conjugate-transpose row vector), 
so that the inner product is literally matrix multiplication: row times 
column gives a scalar.


Incidentally a simpler 'non-conjugate' version of an inner product would miss the mark for positive definiteness. To explore this further: Skip the conjugation when evaluating the norm for $|v\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix}$.


Beyond positive definiteness, an inner product indicates *alignment* or *orthogonality*
or *opposition*. Supposing two vectors $a$ and $b$ point in the same direction on both 
$\mathbb{C}^2$ disks. The inner product should be large as they are "aligned"; and so on for orthogonality and opposition. Without conjugation, two vectors could appear "orthogonal"
for algebraic rather than geometrical reasons. Two additional qualities of the Hermitian adjoint
inner product:


- **Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:** $|\langle a | b \rangle|^2 \leq \langle a|a\rangle \langle b|b\rangle$ 
- **Probability interpretation:** $|\langle a | b \rangle|^2$ gives the probability 
   of measuring state $|a\rangle$ given system is in state $|b\rangle$ (Born rule)



## The fundamental postulate of quantum mechanics


Every *physical state* of a quantum system corresponds to a **ray** in a Hilbert space. 
A ray is an equivalence class of vectors differing only by a nonzero (complex) scalar 
multiplier. After normalizing (Born) the radial component of this multiplier
is gone. The complex phase remains in effect: This is what remains of the
ray, a "global phase angle" that can take on any angular value without
changing the quantum mechanical state. We say 
"a state *is* a unit vector in Hilbert space."


For a single qubit: the Hilbert space is $\mathbb{C}^2$ (two-dimensional, complex). A 
state is a unit vector $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ with 
$|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$.


For a quantum system with more degrees of freedom (e.g. a particle moving in space), 
the Hilbert space is infinite-dimensional ($L^2(\mathbb{R})$, the space of square-integrable 
functions), but the principle is the same: one state = one (ray of) vector(s).


## operators and measurement



There are two types of operators that are regularly applied to states: Gates and 
observables. Gates are necessarily unitary operators and observables are necessarily 
Hermitian operators. Both are applied to quantum states in the course of carrying 
out a quantum calculation. 


This said: There is a separate notion of *measurement* as a *procedure* that invokes 
operators but also introduces probability and irreversibility.

### What is a quantum state, really?


A quantum state is a unit vector in a complex Hilbert space. For a single qubit that 
space is $\mathbb{C}^2$: two complex numbers $\alpha$ and $\beta$ with 
$|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. Written out:

$$|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle = r_\alpha e^{i\eta}|0\rangle + r_\beta e^{i\nu}|1\rangle$$

After factoring out the global phase $e^{i\eta}$ (which changes nothing observable), 
two physical degrees of freedom remain:

- **$\theta$** (colatitude): determined by $r_\alpha = \cos(\theta/2)$. This sets the 
  probability balance: $P(|0\rangle) = \cos^2(\theta/2)$, $P(|1\rangle) = \sin^2(\theta/2)$.
- **$\phi = \nu - \eta$** (relative phase / azimuth): the phase *difference* between 
  the two coefficients.

These two angles parameterize the Bloch sphere. Every point on its surface is a valid 
pure state. The poles are the computational basis states; the equator holds all 
equal-weight superpositions.


### Why complex scalars? The role of interference

The scalar field must be $\mathbb{C}$ (not $\mathbb{R}$) because quantum mechanics 
requires interference — the ability for probability amplitudes to partially cancel.

With real scalars, two amplitudes can only add constructively ($a + b$) or destructively 
($a - b$). With complex scalars, they can combine at any angle: 
$a + e^{i\phi}b$ gives a continuum of interference patterns parameterized by $\phi$. 
This is what the double-slit experiment demonstrates for single particles.

The formal postulate this encodes: a quantum state is a *complex* superposition of 
basis states, and the *squared magnitudes* of the complex amplitudes give probabilities 
(the Born rule). The complex phases between terms produce the interference that 
distinguishes quantum from classical probability.

Without $\mathbb{C}$: no azimuthal angle on the Bloch sphere, no complementary bases, 
no Fourier duality, no quantum computational advantage.
