## Spadework


To cut to the chase, then: Most quantum computing algorithms follow a
standard workflow: An execution plan of 'initialize - transform - measure - repeat'
- Initialization means establishing a quantum system
    - This is in the form of some number of quantum bits (qubits)
- Transformation is the application of a series of discrete logical operations call *gates*
    - The application of these gates effects a *time evolution* of the quantum system
- Measurement resolves the qubits into classical binary states
- Repetition accumulates measurements many times to arrive at a probability distribution


There are refinements of and exceptions to this strategy; but for now
the narrative will build towards this standard workflow, starting with 
some spadework of the 'terms defined without a great deal of explanation' variety.



### The Pauli spin matrices


In quantum computing we work with qubits understood to have two
measureable states. We assert without proof that a number of 2 x 2 matrices 
will play an important role. Here we introduce three 2x2 unitary 
Hermitian matrices -- the Pauli spin matrices -- in terms of components


$$\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad 
\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad 
\sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$


Each has eigenvalues +1 and −1. Their eigenvectors define three mutually 
complementary bases for a single qubit (the X, Y, and Z bases). They generate 
rotations on the Bloch sphere (described below) about the x, y, and z axes 
respectively. 


### Unitary and Hermitian matrices: definitions


**Hermitian (self-adjoint):** A matrix $A$ is Hermitian if it equals its own conjugate 
transpose: $A = A^\dagger$, where $A^\dagger$ is obtained by transposing $A$ and taking 
the complex conjugate of every entry.


- All eigenvalues are **real**
    - In QM observables must be Hermitian — measurement outcomes are real numbers
- Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are mutually orthogonal
- This is easily verified by inspection for the Pauli matrices


**Unitary:** A matrix $U$ is unitary if its conjugate transpose is its inverse:
$U^\dagger U = U U^\dagger = I$.


In 3B1B Linear Algebra this relates to 'what happens to the unit cell upon transform?'


- $U$ **preserves norms**: $\|U|\psi\rangle\| = \||\psi\rangle\|$. 
    - A unit vector stays a unit vector. 
    - This is why gates must be unitary — they preserve the normalization (total probability = 1).
- $U$ **preserves inner products**: $\langle U\phi | U\psi \rangle = \langle \phi | \psi \rangle$.
    - Angles and orthogonality are unchanged.
- Geometrically: unitary = rotation (possibly with reflection) of the Hilbert space.
    - No stretching or squishing.


Example: $\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Compute 
$\sigma_x^\dagger \sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I$.


**The connection:** Unitary operators are the allowed *time evolutions* and *gates* 
(they preserve probability). Hermitian operators are the allowed *observables* (they 
have real measurement outcomes). The Pauli matrices happen to satisfy both conditions 
simultaneously, which is unusual and useful.


## Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)


The DFT operates on an n-dimensional vector producing a new n-vector. Each element
of this new vector is a weighted sum of the elements of the original vector. Hence
we need two indices: $k$ as the index of an element of the output vector and $j$
when indexing the elements of the input vector.


With symmetric $1/\sqrt{2}$ normalization and $n = 2$:


$$X_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \sum_{j=0}^{1} x_j \, e^{-\pi i \, jk}$$


For $k = 0$: the phasor is $e^0 = 1$ for both $j = 0$ and $j = 1$. 
So $X_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x_0 + x_1)$.


For $k = 1$: the phasor is $e^0 = 1$ for $j = 0$, and $e^{-\pi i} = -1$ for $j = 1$. 
So $X_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x_0 - x_1)$.


Written as a matrix:


$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$


In quantum mechanics this is the Hadamard gate: The 2-point DFT with symmetric normalization.


## What is the basis in this situation?

There are two bases in play, and the DFT/Hadamard is the map between them:

**The "time domain" basis** is the standard/computational basis: 
$\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ — the vectors $(1,0)^T$ and $(0,1)^T$. 
When we write a state as $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$, the components 
$\alpha$ and $\beta$ are "time-domain samples." In quantum computing this is 
the Z-basis — the eigenbasis of $\sigma_z$.

**The "frequency domain" basis** is $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$ — the vectors 
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1)^T$ and $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,-1)^T$. These are the 
rows of the Hadamard matrix (equivalently, the DFT basis vectors — the phasors 
themselves). In quantum computing this is the X-basis — the eigenbasis of $\sigma_x$.

**The key insight:** the DFT basis vectors (the phasors you sum against) *become* the 
new basis. When you compute the DFT, you project your vector onto each phasor — and 
those projections are the components in the frequency basis. The Hadamard/DFT doesn't 
create a new vector; it re-expresses the *same* vector in terms of these phasor-basis states.

So the "time domain" / "frequency domain" distinction is just a change of basis in 
$\mathbb{C}^2$. The Hadamard is the unitary matrix that implements this change. This 
is the same structure as position/momentum in continuous QM, shrunk down to two dimensions.


## The Z-basis as the eigenbasis of $\sigma_z$

Recall $\sigma_z$ from the Spadework section. Its eigenvectors satisfy 
$\sigma_z |v\rangle = \lambda |v\rangle$:

- $\sigma_z |0\rangle = (+1)|0\rangle$ — eigenvalue +1
- $\sigma_z |1\rangle = (-1)|1\rangle$ — eigenvalue −1

So "the Z-basis is the eigenbasis of $\sigma_z$" means: $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ are the 
vectors that $\sigma_z$ leaves unchanged in direction (scaled by their eigenvalue).

Structurally $|0\rangle = (1,0)^T$ and $|1\rangle = (0,1)^T$ are the standard unit vectors, 
analogous to $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. The crucial difference: here they 
span $\mathbb{C}^2$, not $\mathbb{R}^2$. The scalars are complex numbers.


## Complex amplitudes

A general single-qubit state is:

$\begin{align}|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle, \quad \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}, \quad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1
\end{align}$


$\begin{align}|0\rangle = 1 \cdot |0\rangle + 0 \cdot |1\rangle
\end{align}$


$\begin{align}|1\rangle = 0 \cdot |0\rangle + 1 \cdot |1\rangle
\end{align}$




The complex nature of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is essential — not a generalization but a 
requirement. States with the same *magnitudes* of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ but different 
*relative phases* are physically distinct:

- $|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$ — relative phase 0
- $|-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle)$ — relative phase $\pi$
- $|{+i}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + i|1\rangle)$ — relative phase $\pi/2$

These are three different points on the Bloch sphere equator: X, −X, and Y directions 
respectively. Without complex scalars, the Y-axis vanishes and you cannot represent the 
full state space.


## From $\mathbb{R}^2$ to $\mathbb{C}^2$: Why the scalar field matters

Consider $\sigma_z$ first in $\mathbb{R}^2$. It acts as reflection across the x-axis: 
$(x, y)^T \to (x, -y)^T$. The eigenvectors are directions unchanged (up to sign) by the 
transform: $\hat{i} = (1,0)^T$ lies along the mirror (eigenvalue +1) and 
$\hat{j} = (0,1)^T$ is perpendicular to it (eigenvalue −1, i.e. flipped).

Now transition to $\mathbb{C}^2$. The basis vectors are written the same: 
$(1,0)^T$ and $(0,1)^T$. But visualize them now on $C^0$ and $C^1$, i.e.
in terms of coefficints $\alpha$ and $\beta$: ... hmmm ... sorta lost the thread here.


The eigenvector/eigenvalue structure of $\sigma_z$ "is unchanged" but chase this down too.
What changes is the **scalar field** supplying the coefficients:

| | $\mathbb{R}^2$ | $\mathbb{C}^2$ |
|---|---|---|
| Scalar field | $\mathbb{R}$ | $\mathbb{C}$ |
| Basis vectors | $(1,0)^T$, $(0,1)^T$ | $(1,0)^T$, $(0,1)^T$ |
| General vector | $a\hat{i} + b\hat{j}$, two real params | $\alpha\|0\rangle + \beta\|1\rangle$, four real params |
| $\sigma_z$ eigenvalues | +1, −1 | +1, −1 |
| Non-eigenvector states | $(a,b)$ with both nonzero | $(\alpha,\beta)$ with complex phases — richer |

The basis vectors *themselves* don't change — what changes is the set of states they can 
span. With complex scalars you can form $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}|1\rangle)$ 
for arbitrary phase $\phi$. These states have no analog in $\mathbb{R}^2$ because $e^{i\phi}$ is not a 
real number.

The physics demands $\mathbb{C}$ because interference requires phase. Two amplitudes equal 
in magnitude but differing in phase can cancel (destructive interference) or reinforce 
(constructive interference). This continuum of partial interference is experimentally 
observed and cannot be reproduced with real scalars alone.


## Visualizing global phase degeneracy


### From α, β to θ, φ, γ — slowly

Starting from the general single-qubit state established above 
($|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ with $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$), 
we now decompose α and β to reveal the Bloch sphere parameters.

Since α and β are complex, write them in polar form:

$$\alpha = r_\alpha \, e^{i\eta}, \quad \beta = r_\beta \, e^{i\nu}$$

where $r_\alpha, r_\beta \geq 0$ are magnitudes and $\eta, \nu$ are phase angles. 
Normalization says $r_\alpha^2 + r_\beta^2 = 1$.

Now factor out the phase of α from the entire state:

$$|\psi\rangle = r_\alpha e^{i\eta}|0\rangle + r_\beta e^{i\nu}|1\rangle = e^{i\eta}\left(r_\alpha|0\rangle + r_\beta e^{i(\nu - \eta)}|1\rangle\right)$$

Define:

- **γ = η** — this is the global phase (the phase we factored out)
- **φ = ν − η** — this is the relative phase (the difference between the two component phases)
- Since $r_\alpha^2 + r_\beta^2 = 1$, parameterize: $r_\alpha = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}$, 
  $r_\beta = \sin\frac{\theta}{2}$ for some $\theta \in [0, \pi]$

Result:

$$|\psi\rangle = e^{i\gamma}\left(\cos\frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle\right)$$

So to summarize the translation:

| Original | Bloch colatitude θ | Bloch azimuth φ | Not appearing (global phase γ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $r_\alpha$ (magnitude of α) | $r_\alpha = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}$ | — | — |
| $r_\beta$ (magnitude of β) | $r_\beta = \sin\frac{\theta}{2}$ | — | — |
| η (phase angle of α) | — | — | γ = η (unobservable) |
| ν (phase angle of β) | — | φ = ν − η | (absorbed into φ and γ) |

The magnitudes of α and β jointly determine θ — how far the state is from the north pole.
The *difference* of their phase angles gives φ — the azimuthal position.
The *absolute* phase angle of α becomes γ — invisible on the Bloch sphere.


### Two complex planes: visualizing α and β

You can visualize the state as a point in *two* complex planes simultaneously:

**Plane 1 (α):** Draw a unit circle. Place α somewhere on or inside it at radius 
$r_\alpha$ and angle η. This fixes the "how much |0⟩" and "what phase for the |0⟩ 
component."

**Plane 2 (β):** Draw another unit circle. Place β at radius $r_\beta$ and angle ν. 
This fixes the "how much |1⟩" and "what phase for the |1⟩ component."

The constraint $r_\alpha^2 + r_\beta^2 = 1$ means: if you fix $r_\alpha$, then 
$r_\beta = \sqrt{1 - r_\alpha^2}$. So β's radius is determined by α's radius. But 
their angles η and ν are independent — giving two free angular parameters.

Now the key decomposition:

- **θ** (polar angle on Bloch sphere) is determined by $r_\alpha$ alone: 
  $\theta = 2\arccos(r_\alpha)$. Since $r_\beta = \sqrt{1 - r_\alpha^2}$, knowing one 
  radius fixes the other — so equivalently θ encodes the *balance* between the two 
  components. North pole: $r_\alpha = 1, r_\beta = 0$. South pole: $r_\alpha = 0, r_\beta = 1$. 
  Equator: $r_\alpha = r_\beta = 1/\sqrt{2}$.

- **φ** (azimuthal angle on Bloch sphere) is the *difference* of the two plane angles: 
  $\phi = \nu - \eta$. This is physically meaningful — it's measurable.

- **γ** (global phase, unobservable) is the *absolute* angle η. You can rotate both 
  points in their respective planes by the same angle and nothing physical changes.


### Why is φ azimuthal when the basis vectors are along z?

This is a reasonable confusion. The basis vectors |0⟩ and |1⟩ sit at the north and south 
poles — separated by polar angle π along the z-axis. There's no azimuthal content in the 
basis vectors themselves. So where does the azimuthal angle come from?

The answer: φ is not an angle *between* the basis vectors. It's the *relative phase* 
between the *coefficients* in the superposition. When both coefficients are nonzero 
(i.e. the state is a superposition, not a pure basis state), the relative phase determines 
*which* superposition you have among the continuum of equal-weight superpositions.

Geometrically on the Bloch sphere:

- At the poles (pure |0⟩ or pure |1⟩), one coefficient is zero so φ is undefined — 
  there's no azimuthal position. This is like the longitude being undefined at 
  Earth's geographic poles.
- On the equator ($\theta = \pi/2$, i.e. $r_\alpha = r_\beta = 1/\sqrt{2}$), φ sweeps 
  you through notable states that form two additional bases:
    - $|+\rangle$ (φ = 0) and $|-\rangle$ (φ = π) — these are the **X-basis** 
      (eigenstates of $\sigma_x$)
    - $|{+i}\rangle$ (φ = π/2) and $|{-i}\rangle$ (φ = 3π/2) — these are the **Y-basis** 
      (eigenstates of $\sigma_y$)
  
  Together with the Z-basis {|0⟩, |1⟩} at the poles, these give three mutually 
  complementary bases — one for each Pauli matrix, one for each axis of the Bloch sphere.

At intermediate latitudes, φ still sweeps a circle but the states are no longer 
  equal-weight superpositions.
  

So φ parameterizes *which direction the superposition points* in the xy-plane of the 
Bloch sphere. It's a property of the superposition coefficients, not of the basis vectors.


### Is global phase visible on the Bloch sphere?

No — and that's the point. The Bloch sphere has already had global phase removed. 
Every point on the Bloch sphere represents an *equivalence class* of states that differ 
only by global phase. The sphere is a 2D surface (parameterized by θ and φ) precisely 
because the third parameter (γ) has been declared unphysical and projected away.

If you want to visualize where γ "went": it's the invisible extra dimension at each point 
of the sphere. The full state space is 3-dimensional (as a real manifold): θ, φ, and γ. 
The Bloch sphere is the 2D *shadow* you get when you ignore γ.


### An equivalent picture: the 3-sphere $S^3$ in four dimensions

Everything above was developed using two complex planes with a normalization constraint.
There is an equivalent, more abstract way to say the same thing: the set of all normalized 
single-qubit states forms $S^3$, the **3-sphere**, which lives in **four-dimensional** space.

To be precise about what this means — and to avoid confusion with an ordinary ball in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

- $S^1$ (a circle) is a 1D curve embedded in $\mathbb{R}^2$. It is the set of points at unit distance from the origin in 2D.
- $S^2$ (an ordinary sphere, like a basketball) is a 2D surface embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$. It is the set of points at unit distance from the origin in 3D.
- $S^3$ is a **3D hypersurface** embedded in $\mathbb{R}^4$. It is the set of points at unit distance from the origin in 4D.

$S^3$ cannot be visualized as a single object in 3D space. It is not a solid ball, not a 
sphere you can hold — it is inherently four-dimensional.

**Why it appears here:** A single-qubit state $(\alpha, \beta) \in \mathbb{C}^2$ has four 
real components (since $\mathbb{C}^2 \cong \mathbb{R}^4$). The normalization constraint 
$|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$ restricts the state to the set of unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^4$ — 
which is exactly the definition of $S^3$.

**Connection to the two-planes picture:** the two complex planes (one for α, one for β) 
with the constraint $r_\alpha^2 + r_\beta^2 = 1$ are a concrete coordinatization of $S^3$. 
They are two different ways of saying the same thing: one geometric (a hypersurface in 4D), 
one computational (two linked complex planes).

**From $S^3$ to the Bloch sphere:** the global phase degeneracy — rotating both α and β 
by the same angle (η → η + δ, ν → ν + δ) — leaves the physics unchanged because 
φ = ν − η is invariant. That synchronized rotation traces a circle within $S^3$. Collapsing 
each such circle to a single point reduces $S^3$ (3-dimensional) to $S^2$ (2-dimensional) — 
the Bloch sphere. In the language of topology this is the **Hopf fibration**: 
$S^3 \to S^2$ with circular fibers, and the Bloch sphere is the base space.


### Summary of the logical chain

$$\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C} \xrightarrow{\text{polar form}} r_\alpha e^{i\eta}, \, r_\beta e^{i\nu} \xrightarrow{\text{normalize}} r_\alpha^2 + r_\beta^2 = 1$$

$$\xrightarrow{\text{parameterize radii}} \theta \xrightarrow{\text{take phase difference}} \phi = \nu - \eta \xrightarrow{\text{discard absolute phase}} \gamma = \eta \text{ (unobservable)}$$

$$\xrightarrow{\text{Bloch sphere}} (\theta, \phi) \text{ specifies the physical state}$$


### Convention: global phase of eigenstates

When we write the eigenstates of $\sigma_x$ as:

$$|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle), \quad |-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle)$$

the global phase has been set to zero **by convention** — meaning α is chosen to be real 
and positive ($\eta = 0$). This is not physics but a choice of representative from the 
equivalence class of states that differ only by global phase.

For $|-\rangle$ specifically: $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{2}$ (real, positive) and 
$\beta = -1/\sqrt{2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e^{i\pi}$. So $r_\beta = 1/\sqrt{2}$ and 
$\nu = \pi$. The relative phase is $\phi = \nu - \eta = \pi - 0 = \pi$, placing it at the 
−x point on the Bloch sphere.

The general principle: eigenvectors are only determined up to a scalar multiple by the 
eigenvalue equation $A|v\rangle = \lambda|v\rangle$. We conventionally normalize (magnitude 1) 
and fix the global phase (first nonzero component real and positive). This selects one 
representative from the degenerate circle of physically identical states.


### Invariance, symmetry, and conservation

Noether's theorem: every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a 
conserved quantity. How does this connect to the phase structure of a qubit?

**Global phase symmetry → probability conservation:**

- **Symmetry:** physics is invariant under $|\psi\rangle \to e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle$
- **Conserved quantity:** total probability ($|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$ is preserved 
  under all unitary time evolution). In quantum field theory, this same U(1) symmetry 
  gives conservation of electric charge.

This is a genuine Noether-type connection.

**Relative phase φ is not a symmetry:**

The relative phase φ = ν − η is a *dynamical variable*, not a symmetry. Different values 
of φ correspond to different physical states with different measurement outcomes. φ can 
change under time evolution (any Hamiltonian with a $\sigma_z$ component will rotate φ).

**φ is gauge-invariant but not conserved:**

The statement "φ is invariant under global phase shifts" means: φ is a well-defined 
physical observable that does not depend on the arbitrary choice of γ convention. This 
is a statement about the *definition* of φ being robust (gauge-invariant), not a 
conservation law.

| | Symmetry/invariance | Associated conservation |
|---|---|---|
| Global phase γ | Physics unchanged under $\|\psi\rangle \to e^{i\gamma}\|\psi\rangle$ | Total probability / charge |
| Relative phase φ | Not a symmetry — different φ = different physics | Not conserved; evolves in time |
| φ invariant under γ-shifts | φ is gauge-invariant (well-defined physical quantity) | Not a conservation law |



## The qubit as the atomic unit of quantum computing


### Is the qubit the only fundamental building block?

Essentially yes. In the current landscape of quantum computing, the qubit (a two-level 
quantum system, Hilbert space $\mathbb{C}^2$) is the universal atomic unit. All major 
platforms — IBM, Google, IonQ, Quantinuum, Rigetti, QuEra — are built on qubits.

There is research into **qutrits** ($\mathbb{C}^3$, three-level systems) and more generally 
**qudits** ($\mathbb{C}^d$, d-level systems), but these are not commercially available and 
remain a niche research direction. For practical purposes: qubit = atom of quantum 
computation.


### Multi-qubit systems build on the single-qubit foundation

Yes — and the construction is precise. Once you understand a single qubit 
(state in $\mathbb{C}^2$, Bloch sphere, gates as rotations), multi-qubit systems are 
built via the **tensor product**:

- 1 qubit: Hilbert space is $\mathbb{C}^2$ (dimension 2)
- 2 qubits: Hilbert space is $\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 = \mathbb{C}^4$ (dimension 4)
- 3 qubits: $\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 = \mathbb{C}^8$ (dimension 8)
- n qubits: $(\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes n} = \mathbb{C}^{2^n}$ (dimension $2^n$)

The state of an n-qubit system is a unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^{2^n}$. The basis states 
are all $2^n$ binary strings: for 2 qubits, $\{|00\rangle, |01\rangle, |10\rangle, |11\rangle\}$.

A general 2-qubit state is:

$$|\psi\rangle = \alpha_{00}|00\rangle + \alpha_{01}|01\rangle + \alpha_{10}|10\rangle + \alpha_{11}|11\rangle$$

with $\sum |\alpha_{ij}|^2 = 1$.


### What's new in multi-qubit systems: entanglement

The single-qubit Bloch sphere picture does **not** straightforwardly generalize to 
multiple qubits. A 2-qubit state lives in $\mathbb{C}^4$, which is not representable as 
two independent Bloch spheres. The reason: **entanglement**.

A state like $|00\rangle$ is a **product state** — it can be written as 
$|0\rangle \otimes |0\rangle$. Each qubit has its own independent state.

But the Bell state $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$ is **entangled** — it 
cannot be factored into a product of individual qubit states. The qubits do not have 
independent descriptions. This is what makes quantum computing powerful (and what makes 
the state space grow exponentially).

So: the single qubit is the atom, the tensor product is how atoms combine, and 
entanglement is the qualitatively new phenomenon that emerges when they do.


## What does the Hadamard gate actually move?

**Claim to evaluate:** When I apply a Hadamard gate, the Bloch sphere doesn't move, the 
state vector doesn't move — rather the basis switches. Apply it again and you're back.

**Verdict:** This is a valid perspective, but there's an equally valid complementary one. 
Which you use depends on whether you adopt the "active" or "passive" interpretation of 
the transformation.


### Active picture (state moves, basis stays)

In the standard operational view of quantum computing: the **state moves** and the 
**basis stays fixed**. The computational basis $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ is nailed down 
(it's what your measurement apparatus reads out). The Hadamard gate rotates the state 
vector on the Bloch sphere — specifically a 180° rotation about the axis halfway between 
x and z.

- Start at north pole ($|0\rangle$) → H sends it to the +x equator ($|+\rangle$)
- The state physically moved on the sphere; the basis didn't change
- Apply H again → the state rotates back to the north pole ($|0\rangle$)

This is how circuit diagrams are conventionally read: gates transform states.


### Passive picture (state stays, basis moves)

Your interpretation is the **passive** picture: the state vector is a fixed abstract 
object in Hilbert space, and the Hadamard changes which basis you use to describe it. 
Before H, you're expressing the state in the Z-basis. After H, you're expressing the 
*same* state in the X-basis.

- Before: state described as $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ (Z-basis components)
- After H: same state, now described as $\alpha'|+\rangle + \beta'|-\rangle$ → but 
  re-expressed in the Z-basis this gives different coefficients
- Apply H again: back to the original description

In this view the Hadamard is a change of coordinate system, not a physical rotation.


### Why both are valid

This is the same active/passive duality that appears throughout physics (rotating the 
object vs. rotating the coordinate axes). Mathematically they are equivalent — a unitary 
transformation $U$ acting on a state ($U|\psi\rangle$) produces the same measurement 
predictions as the inverse transformation acting on the measurement basis.

In quantum computing, the **active picture is standard** because:
- Circuits are sequences of operations that transform states
- Measurement is always in the computational (Z) basis at the end
- The basis is physically defined by the measurement apparatus

But the passive picture gives better intuition for *why* $H^2 = I$: switching from Z-basis 
to X-basis and back is a round trip that returns you to where you started. It also 
clarifies why H appears at the beginning of many algorithms — you're not "doing something 
to the qubit" so much as "choosing to look at it in the frequency domain."


### Summary

| | Active picture | Passive picture |
|---|---|---|
| What moves? | State vector rotates | Basis rotates |
| What's fixed? | Basis (Z-basis) | State vector |
| H on $\|0\rangle$ | State moves to $\|+\rangle$ | Description changes from Z to X |
| $H^2 = I$ | Rotation by 180° twice = 360° = identity | Two basis changes = round trip |
| Standard in QC? | Yes | Less common, but valid for intuition |


## Pauli gates: rotations on the Bloch sphere


Each Pauli matrix, applied as a gate, performs a **180° rotation** (π radians) about its 
respective axis on the Bloch sphere:


- $\sigma_x$ (X gate): 180° about the x-axis
- $\sigma_y$ (Y gate): 180° about the y-axis
- $\sigma_z$ (Z gate): 180° about the z-axis


This is always 180° regardless of where the state starts on the sphere.


### Concrete examples for $\sigma_x$ (rotation about x-axis by 180°):


| Start | End | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| $\|0\rangle$ (north pole) | $\|1\rangle$ (south pole) | Flipped through the equator |
| $\|1\rangle$ (south pole) | $\|0\rangle$ (north pole) | Flipped back |
| $\|+\rangle$ (+x axis) | $\|+\rangle$ (+x axis) | On the rotation axis — unchanged |
| $\|-\rangle$ (−x axis) | $\|-\rangle$ (−x axis) | On the rotation axis — unchanged |
| $\|{+i}\rangle$ (+y axis) | $\|{-i}\rangle$ (−y axis) | Flipped across the xz-plane |


### Why 180°?


Each Pauli matrix satisfies $\sigma^2 = I$ (applying it twice gives the identity). A 
rotation that returns to the identity after two applications must be 180° — since 
180° + 180° = 360° = full rotation = identity.


For rotations by *other* angles, you use the **rotation operators**:


$$R_x(\theta) = e^{-i\theta\sigma_x/2} = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}I - i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sigma_x$$


This rotates by angle $\theta$ about the x-axis (and similarly for $R_y$, $R_z$). The 
Pauli gate itself is the special case $\theta = \pi$:


$$R_x(\pi) = -i\sigma_x$$


(equal to $\sigma_x$ up to global phase, hence the same physical operation).


So the Pauli matrices are the "half-turn" gates. For finer rotations you exponentiate 
them with a continuous parameter.


## Pauli matrices: dual role as gate and observable

When a Pauli matrix is applied to a state ($\sigma_x|\psi\rangle$), it is acting as a 
**linear operator** — a map from the Hilbert space to itself. The term "operator" is 
correct and precise.

As noted in the Spadework section, the Pauli matrices serve two distinct roles:

- As a **gate**: apply $\sigma_x|\psi\rangle$ to transform the state 
  (e.g. $\sigma_x|0\rangle = |1\rangle$, a bit flip). This uses their unitary property.
- As an **observable**: measure $\sigma_z$ and get eigenvalue +1 (collapse to $|0\rangle$) 
  or −1 (collapse to $|1\rangle$). This uses their Hermitian property.

Same matrix, two interpretations — "where does it send the state?" (gate) vs. "what are 
its eigenvalues?" (observable).



## Density matrix, energy eigenstates, and decoherence


### The density matrix

For a pure state $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$, the density matrix is
the outer product:

$$\rho = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi| = \begin{pmatrix} |\alpha|^2 & \alpha\beta^* \\ \alpha^*\beta & |\beta|^2 \end{pmatrix}$$

The diagonal elements ($|\alpha|^2$, $|\beta|^2$) are the measurement probabilities ---
they tell you "how much $|0\rangle$" and "how much $|1\rangle$." The off-diagonal elements
($\alpha\beta^*$ and its conjugate) encode the *coherence* --- the phase relationship
between the two components. They are what make interference possible. If the off-diagonals
are zero, you have a *classical mixture* (the qubit is in $|0\rangle$ OR $|1\rangle$ with
some probability, but not in superposition). If they are nonzero, you have genuine quantum
superposition and interference can occur.

The density matrix is necessary (beyond the state vector) when you have incomplete
knowledge --- either a statistical ensemble of states, or a qubit entangled with an
environment you cannot access.


### Energy of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$

For a physical qubit, $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are energy eigenstates with distinct
energies. In IBM's transmon qubits (superconducting circuits), the energy splitting is
engineered to be $E_1 - E_0 = h\nu$ where $\nu \approx 5$ GHz, giving a gap of roughly
$2 \times 10^{-24}$ J ($\sim 20$ $\mu$eV). This is why they operate at 15 millikelvin ---
thermal energy $k_BT$ must be far below the gap to prevent spontaneous excitation.

A qubit is correctly understood as a proxy for *any* two-state quantum system. The
abstraction is universal. But every physical *implementation* of that abstraction has a
specific energy structure:

| Implementation | $|0\rangle$ | $|1\rangle$ | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transmon (IBM) | ground state of LC oscillator | first excited state | $\sim$5 GHz |
| Trapped ion | ground-state hyperfine level | excited hyperfine level | $\sim$GHz |
| Photon polarization | horizontal | vertical | 0 (degenerate) |
| Spin-1/2 in B field | spin-down (lower) | spin-up (higher) | $\gamma B$ |

The energy gap is what makes $T_1$ decay directional: the qubit preferentially relaxes
*from* $|1\rangle$ *to* $|0\rangle$ (emitting a photon into the environment), not the
reverse. This is exactly like a two-level atom decaying from its excited state.


### Decoherence on the Bloch sphere

- **$T_1$ (energy relaxation):** The Bloch vector shrinks *toward the north pole*
  $(0, 0, 1)$. The state drifts toward $|0\rangle$ regardless of where it started.
  Geometrically: the vector's $z$-component increases toward $+1$ while its length
  contracts.
- **$T_2$ (dephasing):** The Bloch vector shrinks *toward the $z$-axis*. The $x$ and $y$
  components decay while $z$ is preserved. The state loses its azimuthal angle (phase)
  but retains its measurement probabilities. Geometrically: the point stays at the same
  latitude but spirals inward to the axis.
- **Full decoherence ($T_1 + T_2$):** The Bloch vector collapses to a point near
  $(0, 0, 1)$ --- a classical mixture biased toward $|0\rangle$.


### Decoherence on the $\mathbb{C}^2$ double-disk diagram

- **$T_2$ dephasing:** The second disk's dot (the $\beta$ coefficient) *rotates randomly*
  --- its angle becomes uncertain while its magnitude stays fixed. One would draw it as a
  smeared arc rather than a point. The first disk's dot is unaffected.
- **$T_1$ relaxation:** The second disk's dot shrinks toward the origin (losing magnitude)
  while the first disk's dot grows toward $1 + 0i$. Probability flows from $|1\rangle$ to
  $|0\rangle$.
- **Full decoherence:** First disk has a dot at $1 + 0i$, second disk has a dot at the
  origin --- the state $|0\rangle$, with no phase information surviving.


### The interior of the Bloch sphere

The key point: decoherence does not move you to a random pure state on the Bloch sphere
surface. It moves you *inside* the sphere (a mixed state). Points on the surface are pure
states; points inside are classical mixtures. The density matrix formalism is needed
precisely to describe these interior points, which the state vector $|\psi\rangle$ cannot
represent.
