## Spadework


Most quantum computing algorithms follow an execution plan 'initialize - transform - measure - repeat'.


- Initialization means establishing a quantum system
    - Some number of quantum bits (qubits)
- Transformation is the application of a series of discrete logical operations call *gates*
    - The application of these gates effects *time evolution* of the quantum system
    - What evolves are complex coefficients
    - These define the state of a system of N-dimensions...
    - ...written as an $N \times 1$ column vector
- Measurement resolves the qubits into classical binary states
- Repetition accumulates measurements many times to arrive at a probability distribution


This narrative builds toward this standard workflow, starting with 
term definition.


- A vector (often a state vector) is an $N \times 1$ column vector of complex numbers. $N$ is the dimension of the Hilbert space with a minimal non-trivial value for one qubit of $N=2$.
- Qubit basis vectors as kets: $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. As vectors:


$\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.


- Define inner product $\langle a|b \rangle$ (math notation: $\langle a, b \rangle$).


$\langle a|b \rangle = {a}^{\dagger} \cdot b$, a matrix product that produces a single complex number.


- Define tensor product: Combines spaces multiplicatively: Exponential growth of dimension.


$\begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix} \otimes \begin{pmatrix} \gamma \\ \delta \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \beta \\ \alpha \delta \\ \gamma \beta \\ \gamma \delta \end{pmatrix}.$


- Unitary matrix preserves magnitude and has the quality $A^{-1} = A^{\dagger}$.
- Hermitian matrix has real eigenvalues (corresponding to measurement of physical quantities) where $A = A^{\dagger}$.


- A Projector is a ket-bra arrangement of basis vectors, for example $P_0 = |0 \rangle \langle 0 |$. These are used to calculate probabilities: The probability of $|0\rangle$ is $p_0 = \langle \psi | P_0 | \psi \rangle$. 


### The Pauli spin matrices


In quantum computing we work with qubits understood to have two
measureable states. We assert without proof that a number of 2 x 2 matrices 
will play an important role. Here we introduce three 2x2 unitary 
Hermitian matrices -- the Pauli spin matrices -- in terms of components


$$\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad 
\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad 
\sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$


Each has eigenvalues +1 and $-1$. Their eigenvectors define three mutually 
complementary bases for a single qubit (the X, Y, and Z bases). They generate 
rotations on the Bloch sphere (described below) about the x, y, and z axes 
respectively. 


### Unitary and Hermitian matrices: definitions


**Hermitian (self-adjoint):** A matrix $A$ is Hermitian if it equals its own conjugate 
transpose: $A = A^\dagger$, where $A^\dagger$ is obtained by transposing $A$ and taking 
the complex conjugate of every entry.


- All eigenvalues are **real**
    - In QM observables must be Hermitian — measurement outcomes are real numbers
- Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are mutually orthogonal
- This is easily verified by inspection for the Pauli matrices


**Unitary:** A matrix $U$ is unitary if its conjugate transpose is its inverse:
$U^\dagger U = U U^\dagger = I$.


In 3B1B Linear Algebra this relates to 'what happens to the unit cell upon transform?'


- $U$ **preserves norms**: $\|U|\psi\rangle\| = \||\psi\rangle\|$. 
    - A unit vector stays a unit vector. 
    - This is why gates must be unitary — they preserve the normalization (total probability = 1).
- $U$ **preserves inner products**: $\langle U\phi | U\psi \rangle = \langle \phi | \psi \rangle$.
    - Angles and orthogonality are unchanged.
- Geometrically: unitary = rotation (possibly with reflection) of the Hilbert space.
    - No stretching or squishing.


Example: $\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Compute 
$\sigma_x^\dagger \sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I$.


**The connection:** Unitary operators are the allowed *time evolutions* and *gates* 
(they preserve probability). Hermitian operators are the allowed *observables* (they 
have real measurement outcomes). The Pauli matrices happen to satisfy both conditions 
simultaneously, which is unusual and useful.


## Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)


The DFT operates on an n-dimensional vector producing a new n-vector. Each element
of this new vector is a weighted sum of the elements of the original vector. Hence
we need two indices: $k$ as the index of an element of the output vector and $j$
when indexing the elements of the input vector.


With symmetric $1/\sqrt{2}$ normalization and $n = 2$:


$$X_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \sum_{j=0}^{1} x_j \, e^{-\pi i \, jk}$$


For $k = 0$: the phasor is $e^0 = 1$ for both $j = 0$ and $j = 1$. 
So $X_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x_0 + x_1)$.


For $k = 1$: the phasor is $e^0 = 1$ for $j = 0$, and $e^{-\pi i} = -1$ for $j = 1$. 
So $X_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x_0 - x_1)$.


Written as a matrix:


$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$


In quantum mechanics this is the Hadamard gate: The 2-point DFT with symmetric normalization.


## What is the basis in this situation?

There are two bases in play, and the DFT/Hadamard is the map between them:

**The "time domain" basis** is the standard/computational basis: 
$\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ — the vectors $(1,0)^T$ and $(0,1)^T$. 
When we write a state as $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$, the components 
$\alpha$ and $\beta$ are "time-domain samples." In quantum computing this is 
the Z-basis — the eigenbasis of $\sigma_z$.

**The "frequency domain" basis** is $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$ — the vectors 
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1)^T$ and $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,-1)^T$. These are the 
rows of the Hadamard matrix (equivalently, the DFT basis vectors — the phasors 
themselves). In quantum computing this is the X-basis — the eigenbasis of $\sigma_x$.

**The key insight:** the DFT basis vectors (the phasors you sum against) *become* the 
new basis. When you compute the DFT, you project your vector onto each phasor — and 
those projections are the components in the frequency basis. The Hadamard/DFT doesn't 
create a new vector; it re-expresses the *same* vector in terms of these phasor-basis states.

So the "time domain" / "frequency domain" distinction is just a change of basis in 
$\mathbb{C}^2$. The Hadamard is the unitary matrix that implements this change. This 
is the same structure as position/momentum in continuous QM, shrunk down to two dimensions.


## The Z-basis as the eigenbasis of $\sigma_z$

Recall $\sigma_z$ from the Spadework section. Its eigenvectors satisfy 
$\sigma_z |v\rangle = \lambda |v\rangle$:

- $\sigma_z |0\rangle = (+1)|0\rangle$ — eigenvalue +1
- $\sigma_z |1\rangle = (-1)|1\rangle$ — eigenvalue $-1$

So "the Z-basis is the eigenbasis of $\sigma_z$" means: $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ are the 
vectors that $\sigma_z$ leaves unchanged in direction (scaled by their eigenvalue).

Structurally $|0\rangle = (1,0)^T$ and $|1\rangle = (0,1)^T$ are the standard unit vectors, 
analogous to $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. The crucial difference: here they 
span $\mathbb{C}^2$, not $\mathbb{R}^2$. The scalars are complex numbers.


## Complex amplitudes

A general single-qubit state is:

$\begin{align}|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle, \quad \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}, \quad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1
\end{align}$


$\begin{align}|0\rangle = 1 \cdot |0\rangle + 0 \cdot |1\rangle
\end{align}$


$\begin{align}|1\rangle = 0 \cdot |0\rangle + 1 \cdot |1\rangle
\end{align}$




The complex nature of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is essential — not a generalization but a 
requirement. States with the same *magnitudes* of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ but different 
*relative phases* are physically distinct:

- $|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$ — relative phase 0
- $|-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle)$ — relative phase $\pi$
- $|{+i}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + i|1\rangle)$ — relative phase $\pi/2$

These are three different points on the Bloch sphere equator: X, $-$X, and Y directions 
respectively. Without complex scalars, the Y-axis vanishes and you cannot represent the 
full state space.


## From $\mathbb{R}^2$ to $\mathbb{C}^2$: Why the scalar field matters

Consider $\sigma_z$ first in $\mathbb{R}^2$. It acts as reflection across the x-axis: 
$(x, y)^T \to (x, -y)^T$. The eigenvectors are directions unchanged (up to sign) by the 
transform: $\hat{i} = (1,0)^T$ lies along the mirror (eigenvalue +1) and 
$\hat{j} = (0,1)^T$ is perpendicular to it (eigenvalue $-1$, i.e. flipped).

Now transition to $\mathbb{C}^2$. The basis vectors are written the same: 
$(1,0)^T$ and $(0,1)^T$. But visualize them now on $C^0$ and $C^1$, i.e.
in terms of coefficints $\alpha$ and $\beta$: ... hmmm ... sorta lost the thread here.


The eigenvector/eigenvalue structure of $\sigma_z$ "is unchanged" but chase this down too.
What changes is the **scalar field** supplying the coefficients:

| | $\mathbb{R}^2$ | $\mathbb{C}^2$ |
|---|---|---|
| Scalar field | $\mathbb{R}$ | $\mathbb{C}$ |
| Basis vectors | $(1,0)^T$, $(0,1)^T$ | $(1,0)^T$, $(0,1)^T$ |
| General vector | $a\hat{i} + b\hat{j}$, two real params | $\alpha\|0\rangle + \beta\|1\rangle$, four real params |
| $\sigma_z$ eigenvalues | +1, $-1$ | +1, $-1$ |
| Non-eigenvector states | $(a,b)$ with both nonzero | $(\alpha,\beta)$ with complex phases — richer |

The basis vectors *themselves* don't change — what changes is the set of states they can 
span. With complex scalars you can form $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}|1\rangle)$ 
for arbitrary phase $\phi$. These states have no analog in $\mathbb{R}^2$ because $e^{i\phi}$ is not a 
real number.

The physics demands $\mathbb{C}$ because interference requires phase. Two amplitudes equal 
in magnitude but differing in phase can cancel (destructive interference) or reinforce 
(constructive interference). This continuum of partial interference is experimentally 
observed and cannot be reproduced with real scalars alone.


## Visualizing global phase degeneracy


### From $\alpha$, $\beta$ to $\theta$, $\varphi$, $\gamma$ — slowly

Starting from the general single-qubit state established above 
($|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ with $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$), 
we now decompose $\alpha$ and $\beta$ to reveal the Bloch sphere parameters.

Since $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are complex, write them in polar form:

$$\alpha = r_\alpha \, e^{i\eta}, \quad \beta = r_\beta \, e^{i\nu}$$

where $r_\alpha, r_\beta \geq 0$ are magnitudes and $\eta, \nu$ are phase angles.
Normalization says $r_\alpha^2 + r_\beta^2 = 1$.

Now factor out the phase of $\alpha$ from the entire state:

$$|\psi\rangle = r_\alpha e^{i\eta}|0\rangle + r_\beta e^{i\nu}|1\rangle = e^{i\eta}\left(r_\alpha|0\rangle + r_\beta e^{i(\nu - \eta)}|1\rangle\right)$$

Define:

- **$\gamma = \eta$** — this is the global phase (the phase we factored out)
- **$\varphi = \nu - \eta$** — this is the relative phase (the difference between the two component phases)
- Since $r_\alpha^2 + r_\beta^2 = 1$, parameterize: $r_\alpha = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}$, 
  $r_\beta = \sin\frac{\theta}{2}$ for some $\theta \in [0, \pi]$

Result:

$$|\psi\rangle = e^{i\gamma}\left(\cos\frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle\right)$$

So to summarize the translation:

| Original | Bloch colatitude $\theta$ | Bloch azimuth $\varphi$ | Not appearing (global phase $\gamma$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $r_\alpha$ (magnitude of $\alpha$) | $r_\alpha = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}$ | — | — |
| $r_\beta$ (magnitude of $\beta$) | $r_\beta = \sin\frac{\theta}{2}$ | — | — |
| $\eta$ (phase angle of $\alpha$) | — | — | $\gamma = \eta$ (unobservable) |
| $\nu$ (phase angle of $\beta$) | — | $\varphi = \nu - \eta$ | (absorbed into $\varphi$ and $\gamma$) |

The magnitudes of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ jointly determine $\theta$ — how far the state is from the north pole.
The *difference* of their phase angles gives $\varphi$ — the azimuthal position.
The *absolute* phase angle of $\alpha$ becomes $\gamma$ — invisible on the Bloch sphere.


### Two complex planes: visualizing $\alpha$ and $\beta$

You can visualize the state as a point in *two* complex planes simultaneously:

**Plane 1 ($\alpha$):** Draw a unit circle. Place $\alpha$ somewhere on or inside it at radius 
$r_\alpha$ and angle $\eta$. This fixes the "how much $|0\rangle$" and "what phase for the $|0\rangle$ 
component."

**Plane 2 ($\beta$):** Draw another unit circle. Place $\beta$ at radius $r_\beta$ and angle $\nu$. 
This fixes the "how much $|1\rangle$" and "what phase for the $|1\rangle$ component."

The constraint $r_\alpha^2 + r_\beta^2 = 1$ means: if you fix $r_\alpha$, then 
$r_\beta = \sqrt{1 - r_\alpha^2}$. So $\beta$'s radius is determined by $\alpha$'s radius. But 
their angles $\eta$ and $\nu$ are independent — giving two free angular parameters.

Now the key decomposition:

- **$\theta$** (polar angle on Bloch sphere) is determined by $r_\alpha$ alone: 
  $\theta = 2\arccos(r_\alpha)$. Since $r_\beta = \sqrt{1 - r_\alpha^2}$, knowing one 
  radius fixes the other — so equivalently $\theta$ encodes the *balance* between the two 
  components. North pole: $r_\alpha = 1, r_\beta = 0$. South pole: $r_\alpha = 0, r_\beta = 1$. 
  Equator: $r_\alpha = r_\beta = 1/\sqrt{2}$.

- **$\varphi$** (azimuthal angle on Bloch sphere) is the *difference* of the two plane angles: 
  $\phi = \nu - \eta$. This is physically meaningful — it's measurable.

- **$\gamma$** (global phase, unobservable) is the *absolute* angle $\eta$. You can rotate both 
  points in their respective planes by the same angle and nothing physical changes.


### Why is $\varphi$ azimuthal when the basis vectors are along z?

This is a reasonable confusion. The basis vectors $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ sit at the north and south 
poles — separated by polar angle $\pi$ along the z-axis. There's no azimuthal content in the 
basis vectors themselves. So where does the azimuthal angle come from?

The answer: $\varphi$ is not an angle *between* the basis vectors. It's the *relative phase* 
between the *coefficients* in the superposition. When both coefficients are nonzero 
(i.e. the state is a superposition, not a pure basis state), the relative phase determines 
*which* superposition you have among the continuum of equal-weight superpositions.

Geometrically on the Bloch sphere:

- At the poles (pure $|0\rangle$ or pure $|1\rangle$), one coefficient is zero so $\varphi$ is undefined — 
  there's no azimuthal position. This is like the longitude being undefined at 
  Earth's geographic poles.
- On the equator ($\theta = \pi/2$, i.e. $r_\alpha = r_\beta = 1/\sqrt{2}$), $\varphi$ sweeps 
  you through notable states that form two additional bases:
    - $|+\rangle$ ($\varphi$ = 0) and $|-\rangle$ ($\varphi = \pi$) — these are the **X-basis** 
      (eigenstates of $\sigma_x$)
    - $|{+i}\rangle$ ($\varphi = \pi/2$) and $|{-i}\rangle$ ($\varphi = 3\pi/2$) — these are the **Y-basis** 
      (eigenstates of $\sigma_y$)
  
  Together with the Z-basis $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ at the poles, these give three mutually 
  complementary bases — one for each Pauli matrix, one for each axis of the Bloch sphere.

At intermediate latitudes, $\varphi$ still sweeps a circle but the states are no longer 
  equal-weight superpositions.
  

So $\varphi$ parameterizes *which direction the superposition points* in the xy-plane of the 
Bloch sphere. It's a property of the superposition coefficients, not of the basis vectors.


### Is global phase visible on the Bloch sphere?

No — and that's the point. The Bloch sphere has already had global phase removed. 
Every point on the Bloch sphere represents an *equivalence class* of states that differ 
only by global phase. The sphere is a 2D surface (parameterized by $\theta$ and $\varphi$) precisely 
because the third parameter ($\gamma$) has been declared unphysical and projected away.

If you want to visualize where $\gamma$ "went": it's the invisible extra dimension at each point 
of the sphere. The full state space is 3-dimensional (as a real manifold): $\theta$, $\varphi$, and $\gamma$. 
The Bloch sphere is the 2D *shadow* you get when you ignore $\gamma$.


### An equivalent picture: the 3-sphere $S^3$ in four dimensions

Everything above was developed using two complex planes with a normalization constraint.
There is an equivalent, more abstract way to say the same thing: the set of all normalized 
single-qubit states forms $S^3$, the **3-sphere**, which lives in **four-dimensional** space.

To be precise about what this means — and to avoid confusion with an ordinary ball in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

- $S^1$ (a circle) is a 1D curve embedded in $\mathbb{R}^2$. It is the set of points at unit distance from the origin in 2D.
- $S^2$ (an ordinary sphere, like a basketball) is a 2D surface embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$. It is the set of points at unit distance from the origin in 3D.
- $S^3$ is a **3D hypersurface** embedded in $\mathbb{R}^4$. It is the set of points at unit distance from the origin in 4D.

$S^3$ cannot be visualized as a single object in 3D space. It is not a solid ball, not a 
sphere you can hold — it is inherently four-dimensional.

**Why it appears here:** A single-qubit state $(\alpha, \beta) \in \mathbb{C}^2$ has four 
real components (since $\mathbb{C}^2 \cong \mathbb{R}^4$). The normalization constraint 
$|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$ restricts the state to the set of unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^4$ — 
which is exactly the definition of $S^3$.

**Connection to the two-planes picture:** the two complex planes (one for $\alpha$, one for $\beta$) 
with the constraint $r_\alpha^2 + r_\beta^2 = 1$ are a concrete coordinatization of $S^3$. 
They are two different ways of saying the same thing: one geometric (a hypersurface in 4D), 
one computational (two linked complex planes).

**From $S^3$ to the Bloch sphere:** the global phase degeneracy — rotating both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ 
by the same angle ($\eta \to \eta + \delta$, $\nu \to \nu + \delta$) — leaves the physics unchanged because 
$\varphi = \nu - \eta$ is invariant. That synchronized rotation traces a circle within $S^3$. Collapsing 
each such circle to a single point reduces $S^3$ (3-dimensional) to $S^2$ (2-dimensional) — 
the Bloch sphere. In the language of topology this is the **Hopf fibration**: 
$S^3 \to S^2$ with circular fibers, and the Bloch sphere is the base space.


### Advantage of the Bloch sphere over the two-disc picture

The two complex unit discs and the Bloch sphere describe the *same* physics, but the discs
carry **redundant** information while the sphere shows only what is physically real. That is
the core advantage: the Bloch sphere is a one-to-one map of distinct physical states, whereas
the two discs are a one-to-many (over)representation.

**Where the redundancy lives.** The discs give four real numbers: $r_\alpha, \eta$ for
$\alpha$ and $r_\beta, \nu$ for $\beta$. Two of the four are not independent physical facts:

- Normalization ties the radii, $r_\alpha^2 + r_\beta^2 = 1$, so one radius is determined by
  the other (one spurious degree of freedom).
- Global phase is unobservable: rotating *both* discs by the same angle changes nothing
  measurable; only the difference $\varphi = \nu - \eta$ is physical (a second spurious
  degree of freedom).

Four parameters minus two redundancies leaves the two physical numbers the sphere displays:
the colatitude $\theta$ (radius balance) and the azimuth $\varphi$ (phase difference).

**Concrete advantages of the sphere:**

- *Faithful uniqueness.* Each physical state is one point and each point is one state. On the
  discs, an entire circle of global-phase-rotated configurations maps to a single physical
  state.
- *Distance is meaningful.* Antipodal points are orthogonal (perfectly distinguishable),
  nearby points are hard to distinguish. The disc picture has no comparably clean
  distinguishability metric, because part of the apparent separation is unphysical
  global-phase rotation.
- *Gates are rotations.* Every single-qubit unitary is a rigid rotation of the sphere (Pauli
  gates are $180^\circ$ flips; $R_x, R_y, R_z$ are arbitrary-angle rotations), so the
  operation is visible. On two discs a gate like Hadamard scrambles both radii and both
  angles in a coupled, non-obvious way.
- *All three bases at once.* Poles (Z-basis) and the $\pm x$, $\pm y$ equatorial points (X-
  and Y-bases) sit on one object, making complementarity geometrically obvious.

**Where the two discs are better.** The trade cuts both ways:

- The discs show the amplitudes *literally* — each coefficient's magnitude and phase. The
  Bloch sphere has discarded the global phase, so it cannot show $\alpha$ and $\beta$ as
  complex numbers at all. When teaching what a superposition *is*, or when the global phase
  still matters, the discs are the right tool.
- The discs make the $4 \to 2$ reduction visible *as a process*: one can watch normalization
  couple the radii and global phase cancel. The sphere presents the end product with that
  reasoning already hidden inside it.

So: two discs are faithful to the raw coefficients but redundant; the Bloch sphere is
faithful to the physical state and minimal. The sphere wins for reasoning about gates,
distinguishability, and dynamics; the discs win for showing what the amplitudes literally are
and for teaching the reduction itself. (This is why `C_to_Bloch.py` animates the discs
*into* the sphere: the discs teach, the sphere summarizes.)


### Summary of the logical chain

$$\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C} \xrightarrow{\text{polar form}} r_\alpha e^{i\eta}, \, r_\beta e^{i\nu} \xrightarrow{\text{normalize}} r_\alpha^2 + r_\beta^2 = 1$$

$$\xrightarrow{\text{parameterize radii}} \theta \xrightarrow{\text{take phase difference}} \phi = \nu - \eta \xrightarrow{\text{discard absolute phase}} \gamma = \eta \text{ (unobservable)}$$

$$\xrightarrow{\text{Bloch sphere}} (\theta, \phi) \text{ specifies the physical state}$$


### Convention: global phase of eigenstates

When we write the eigenstates of $\sigma_x$ as:

$$|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle), \quad |-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle)$$

the global phase has been set to zero **by convention** — meaning $\alpha$ is chosen to be real 
and positive ($\eta = 0$). This is not physics but a choice of representative from the 
equivalence class of states that differ only by global phase.

For $|-\rangle$ specifically: $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{2}$ (real, positive) and 
$\beta = -1/\sqrt{2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e^{i\pi}$. So $r_\beta = 1/\sqrt{2}$ and 
$\nu = \pi$. The relative phase is $\phi = \nu - \eta = \pi - 0 = \pi$, placing it at the 
$-$x point on the Bloch sphere.

The general principle: eigenvectors are only determined up to a scalar multiple by the 
eigenvalue equation $A|v\rangle = \lambda|v\rangle$. We conventionally normalize (magnitude 1) 
and fix the global phase (first nonzero component real and positive). This selects one 
representative from the degenerate circle of physically identical states.


### Invariance, symmetry, and conservation

Noether's theorem: every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a 
conserved quantity. How does this connect to the phase structure of a qubit?

**Global phase symmetry $\to$ probability conservation:**

- **Symmetry:** physics is invariant under $|\psi\rangle \to e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle$
- **Conserved quantity:** total probability ($|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$ is preserved 
  under all unitary time evolution). In quantum field theory, this same U(1) symmetry 
  gives conservation of electric charge.

This is a genuine Noether-type connection.

**Relative phase $\varphi$ is not a symmetry:**

The relative phase $\varphi = \nu - \eta$ is a *dynamical variable*, not a symmetry. Different values 
of $\varphi$ correspond to different physical states with different measurement outcomes. $\varphi$ can 
change under time evolution (any Hamiltonian with a $\sigma_z$ component will rotate $\varphi$).

**$\varphi$ is gauge-invariant but not conserved:**

The statement "$\varphi$ is invariant under global phase shifts" means: $\varphi$ is a well-defined 
physical observable that does not depend on the arbitrary choice of $\gamma$ convention. This 
is a statement about the *definition* of $\varphi$ being robust (gauge-invariant), not a 
conservation law.

| | Symmetry/invariance | Associated conservation |
|---|---|---|
| Global phase $\gamma$ | Physics unchanged under $\|\psi\rangle \to e^{i\gamma}\|\psi\rangle$ | Total probability / charge |
| Relative phase $\varphi$ | Not a symmetry — different $\varphi$ = different physics | Not conserved; evolves in time |
| $\varphi$ invariant under $\gamma$-shifts | $\varphi$ is gauge-invariant (well-defined physical quantity) | Not a conservation law |


## What does the Hadamard gate actually move?

**Claim to evaluate:** When I apply a Hadamard gate, the Bloch sphere doesn't move, the 
state vector doesn't move — rather the basis switches. Apply it again and you're back.

**Verdict:** This is a valid perspective, but there's an equally valid complementary one. 
Which you use depends on whether you adopt the "active" or "passive" interpretation of 
the transformation.


### Active picture (state moves, basis stays)

In the standard operational view of quantum computing: the **state moves** and the 
**basis stays fixed**. The computational basis $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ is nailed down 
(it's what your measurement apparatus reads out). The Hadamard gate rotates the state 
vector on the Bloch sphere — specifically a 180° rotation about the axis halfway between 
x and z.

- Start at north pole ($|0\rangle$) $\to$ H sends it to the +x equator ($|+\rangle$)
- The state physically moved on the sphere; the basis didn't change
- Apply H again $\to$ the state rotates back to the north pole ($|0\rangle$)

This is how circuit diagrams are conventionally read: gates transform states.


### Passive picture (state stays, basis moves)

Your interpretation is the **passive** picture: the state vector is a fixed abstract 
object in Hilbert space, and the Hadamard changes which basis you use to describe it. 
Before H, you're expressing the state in the Z-basis. After H, you're expressing the 
*same* state in the X-basis.

- Before: state described as $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ (Z-basis components)
- After H: same state, now described as $\alpha'|+\rangle + \beta'|-\rangle$ $\to$ but 
  re-expressed in the Z-basis this gives different coefficients
- Apply H again: back to the original description

In this view the Hadamard is a change of coordinate system, not a physical rotation.


### Why both are valid

This is the same active/passive duality that appears throughout physics (rotating the 
object vs. rotating the coordinate axes). Mathematically they are equivalent — a unitary 
transformation $U$ acting on a state ($U|\psi\rangle$) produces the same measurement 
predictions as the inverse transformation acting on the measurement basis.

In quantum computing, the **active picture is standard** because:
- Circuits are sequences of operations that transform states
- Measurement is always in the computational (Z) basis at the end
- The basis is physically defined by the measurement apparatus

But the passive picture gives better intuition for *why* $H^2 = I$: switching from Z-basis 
to X-basis and back is a round trip that returns you to where you started. It also 
clarifies why H appears at the beginning of many algorithms — you're not "doing something 
to the qubit" so much as "choosing to look at it in the frequency domain."


### Summary

| | Active picture | Passive picture |
|---|---|---|
| What moves? | State vector rotates | Basis rotates |
| What's fixed? | Basis (Z-basis) | State vector |
| H on $\|0\rangle$ | State moves to $\|+\rangle$ | Description changes from Z to X |
| $H^2 = I$ | Rotation by 180° twice = 360° = identity | Two basis changes = round trip |
| Standard in QC? | Yes | Less common, but valid for intuition |


## Pauli gates: rotations on the Bloch sphere


Each Pauli matrix, applied as a gate, performs a **180° rotation** ($\pi$ radians) about its 
respective axis on the Bloch sphere:


- $\sigma_x$ (X gate): 180° about the x-axis
- $\sigma_y$ (Y gate): 180° about the y-axis
- $\sigma_z$ (Z gate): 180° about the z-axis


This is always 180° regardless of where the state starts on the sphere.


### Concrete examples for $\sigma_x$ (rotation about x-axis by 180°):


| Start | End | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| $\|0\rangle$ (north pole) | $\|1\rangle$ (south pole) | Flipped through the equator |
| $\|1\rangle$ (south pole) | $\|0\rangle$ (north pole) | Flipped back |
| $\|+\rangle$ (+x axis) | $\|+\rangle$ (+x axis) | On the rotation axis — unchanged |
| $\|-\rangle$ ($-$x axis) | $\|-\rangle$ ($-$x axis) | On the rotation axis — unchanged |
| $\|{+i}\rangle$ (+y axis) | $\|{-i}\rangle$ ($-$y axis) | Flipped across the xz-plane |


### Why 180°?


Each Pauli matrix satisfies $\sigma^2 = I$ (applying it twice gives the identity). A 
rotation that returns to the identity after two applications must be 180° — since 
180° + 180° = 360° = full rotation = identity.


For rotations by *other* angles, you use the **rotation operators**:


$$R_x(\theta) = e^{-i\theta\sigma_x/2} = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}I - i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sigma_x$$


This rotates by angle $\theta$ about the x-axis (and similarly for $R_y$, $R_z$). The 
Pauli gate itself is the special case $\theta = \pi$:


$$R_x(\pi) = -i\sigma_x$$


(equal to $\sigma_x$ up to global phase, hence the same physical operation).


So the Pauli matrices are the "half-turn" gates. For finer rotations you exponentiate 
them with a continuous parameter.


## Pauli matrices: dual role as gate and observable

When a Pauli matrix is applied to a state ($\sigma_x|\psi\rangle$), it is acting as a 
**linear operator** — a map from the Hilbert space to itself. The term "operator" is 
correct and precise.

As noted in the Spadework section, the Pauli matrices serve two distinct roles:

- As a **gate**: apply $\sigma_x|\psi\rangle$ to transform the state 
  (e.g. $\sigma_x|0\rangle = |1\rangle$, a bit flip). This uses their unitary property.
- As an **observable**: measure $\sigma_z$ and get eigenvalue +1 (collapse to $|0\rangle$) 
  or $-1$ (collapse to $|1\rangle$). This uses their Hermitian property.

Same matrix, two interpretations — "where does it send the state?" (gate) vs. "what are 
its eigenvalues?" (observable).


## Measurement: spectral decomposition, the Born rule, and projection

(Developed slowly in a learning-mode session; this section captures the chain
from "a Hermitian matrix is an observable" to "probability = amplitude squared,"
one link at a time.)

### A matrix means different things depending on the formula around it

A matrix is just a grid of numbers with no built-in job. The formula it sits in
decides its role. Plain (non-quantum) example with

$$M = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad v = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Used one way it *transforms* a vector into a new vector:

$$M v = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Used another way it *reports a single number* about a vector:

$$v^\dagger M v = 5.$$

Same matrix. The first formula moves the vector; the second measures something
and hands back a scalar.

This is exactly the two roles of $\sigma_z$:

- **Gate:** $\sigma_z|\psi\rangle$ — matrix times ket gives a new ket. Evolves
  the state, no measurement. Uses unitarity.
- **Observable:** we never compute $\sigma_z|\psi\rangle$. We only read off its
  eigenvalues $(+1, -1)$ and eigenvectors $(|0\rangle, |1\rangle)$. The matrix is
  a *lookup table* of "possible readings" and "which states they belong to." The
  actual measuring is done by projectors and the Born rule, not by $\sigma_z$.

### Why the dagger conjugates: real, non-negative lengths

The inner product lives on an *inner product space* (added structure on top of a
plain vector space — the vector-space axioms never multiply two vectors). Its
axioms (conjugate symmetry, linearity in the second argument, positive
definiteness) force self-overlaps to be real and non-negative. The conjugation
in the dagger is what delivers this. One-element example, $v = (i)$:

$$v^T v = i \cdot i = -1 \quad (\text{broken: negative "length"}),$$

$$v^\dagger v = \overline{i}\cdot i = (-i)(i) = +1 \quad (\text{correct}).$$

In general $\overline{z}\,z = |z|^2 \ge 0$, always real.

### Spectral decomposition: rebuild the matrix from its measurement data

The outer product (column times row, a matrix) of a basis state with itself is a
**projector**:

$$P_0 = |0\rangle\langle 0| = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
P_1 = |1\rangle\langle 1| = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The observable equals its eigenvalues each weighting the projector onto the
matching eigenvector:

$$\sigma_z = (+1)P_0 + (-1)P_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

This is not an approximation or a separate object — it is literally the same
matrix, rebuilt from "what readings are possible (eigenvalues)" and "which states
they belong to (projectors)." Every observable decomposes this way, each in its
own eigenbasis. For $\sigma_x$ the same recipe uses

$$|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1, 1)^T, \qquad |-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1, -1)^T,$$

so $\sigma_x = (+1)|+\rangle\langle +| + (-1)|-\rangle\langle -|$.

### The Born rule as amplitude squared

Put the projector into the Born rule and regroup by associativity:

$$p_0 = \langle\psi|P_0|\psi\rangle = \big(\langle\psi|0\rangle\big)\big(\langle 0|\psi\rangle\big).$$

The two factors are conjugate partners (conjugate symmetry), so this is a number
times its own conjugate:

$$p_0 = \overline{\langle 0|\psi\rangle}\,\langle 0|\psi\rangle = \big|\langle 0|\psi\rangle\big|^2.$$

The single quantity $\langle 0|\psi\rangle$ is the **amplitude** — the projection
of the (wandering) state $|\psi\rangle$ onto the (fixed-reference) basis vector
$|0\rangle$. "Project the state onto the basis" is standard and is also the
operator's-eye view, since $P_0|\psi\rangle = |0\rangle\langle 0|\psi\rangle$:
measure the overlap with the fixed axis, then lay that amount back along the
axis. Bookkeeping note: the amplitude with that meaning is specifically
$\langle 0|\psi\rangle$ (fixed-reference vector as the bra); $\langle\psi|0\rangle$
is its conjugate.

### Every axis mathematically; only z in hardware

The Born rule is basis-agnostic — project onto any observable's eigenvectors and
square:

$$p_+ = \big|\langle +|\psi\rangle\big|^2, \qquad p_{+i} = \big|\langle +i|\psi\rangle\big|^2.$$

But real hardware physically performs only the z (computational-basis) readout.
To measure another axis, rotate it onto z first, then measure z; the rotating
unitary carries the state along, so z-statistics on the rotated state equal the
desired-axis statistics on the original ($\sigma_x$: apply $H$ then measure z;
$\sigma_y$: apply $S^\dagger$ then $H$ then measure z). So the "can't measure
x/y" is "can't *directly*," not "can't at all." The full per-axis recipe, gate
conventions, and the $\sigma_y$ derivation are in **Observables and how to
measure them** below.

### Orthonormal within a basis, not across bases

Distinct vectors *within one basis* are orthogonal:

$$\langle 0|1\rangle = 0, \qquad \langle +|-\rangle = 0, \qquad \langle +i|-i\rangle = 0.$$

Vectors from *different* bases generally are not — and their overlap is exactly
the measurement probability linking the two bases. Example (z-basis $|1\rangle$
against y-basis $|-i\rangle$):

$$\langle 1|-i\rangle = \frac{-i}{\sqrt 2}, \qquad \big|\langle 1|-i\rangle\big|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}.$$

The nonzero value is not an error: a y-axis state on the equator gives a 50/50
z-measurement. For the three Pauli bases every cross-overlap has magnitude
squared $\tfrac{1}{2}$ — they are *mutually unbiased*. (Re-phasing an eigenvector
by an arbitrary global phase leaves within-basis orthonormality untouched.)

### Summary: the three jobs Hermiticity does at once

"Observable is Hermitian, so real eigenvalues" is shorthand for three linked
facts:

1. **Eigenvalues are the measurement outcomes**, and Hermiticity ($A = A^\dagger$)
   guarantees they are real — physical readings (energies, spins, $\pm 1$) must be
   real numbers.
2. **Eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis** — the outcomes are mutually
   exclusive and complete, so the projectors satisfy $P_0 + P_1 = I$ and
   $P_0 P_1 = 0$ and the probabilities sum to 1.
3. **The projectors come from that same operator** via spectral decomposition, so
   $p_k = \langle\psi|P_k|\psi\rangle$: the eigenvalues give the *values*, the
   projectors give the *probabilities*. (For the proof that Hermitian forces real
   eigenvalues, see **Why a symmetric (Hermitian) matrix has real eigenvalues**
   below.)


## Energy and the Hamiltonian

Energy is not represented by a gate. In quantum mechanics energy is an
*observable*, so it is represented by a Hermitian operator, the **Hamiltonian**
$\hat{H}$, not by a unitary gate. The name is inherited from classical mechanics,
where the Hamiltonian function is the total energy written in terms of position
and momentum. Its eigenvalue equation

$$\hat{H}\,|E_n\rangle = E_n\,|E_n\rangle$$

gives the allowed energies $E_n$ (the possible measurement results) and the energy
eigenstates $|E_n\rangle$.

For a single qubit the Hamiltonian is diagonal in the computational basis,
with the ground and first-excited states as its eigenstates:

$$\hat{H}\,|0\rangle = E_0\,|0\rangle, \qquad \hat{H}\,|1\rangle = E_1\,|1\rangle.$$

Measuring energy therefore means measuring in the $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$
basis, with outcomes $E_0$ or $E_1$. This is the same eigenbasis as $\sigma_z$;
up to scaling and an offset, $\hat{H} \propto \sigma_z$ for a two-level system.

### Does "measuring energy" mean measuring $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$?

Not inherently. The states $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are energy eigenstates **only if the
chosen Hamiltonian makes them so**. The labels "0" and "1" carry no energy on their own;
everything depends on what $\hat{H}$ is.

The standard qubit convention (modeling a two-level atom) takes

$$\hat{H} = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\,\hat{Z}
          = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

With this choice $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are the eigenstates:

- $\hat{H}\,|0\rangle = +\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}\,|0\rangle$
- $\hat{H}\,|1\rangle = -\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}\,|1\rangle$

(Sign conventions differ; many texts flip $\hat{Z}$ or add a constant so that $|0\rangle$ is the
ground state. The project's own `C_to_Bloch.py` labels $|0\rangle$ the "ground state," i.e. the
opposite sign from the bare $\hat{Z}$ above — purely a convention choice.)

So:

- **Measuring energy** = measuring the observable $\hat{H}$. When $\hat{H}\propto\hat{Z}$, its
  eigenbasis *is* the computational basis $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$, so an energy measurement and
  a computational-basis ($\hat{Z}$) measurement are the same act and return one of two energies.
- A general state $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ collapses to $|0\rangle$ (energy
  $+\hbar\omega/2$) with probability $|\alpha|^2$, or to $|1\rangle$ (energy $-\hbar\omega/2$) with
  probability $|\beta|^2$.

The subtlety: "measuring energy" coincides with "measuring in the $|0\rangle / |1\rangle$ basis"
**only when the Hamiltonian is diagonal in that basis**. If instead $\hat{H}\propto\hat{X}$, the
energy eigenstates are $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$, and an energy measurement would not correspond
to asking whether the outcome was $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$.


## Time evolution $U(t) = e^{-i\hat H t/\hbar}$

There is no single "energy gate," but energy drives the unitary that advances the
system in time. The link from the Hamiltonian (energy) to the gates of a quantum
circuit is the time-dependent Schrodinger equation,

$$i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{H}\,|\psi(t)\rangle.$$

For a time-independent $\hat{H}$ this first-order linear equation integrates just like
$\frac{dy}{dt}=ky \Rightarrow y=e^{kt}y_0$, but with an operator in the exponent:

$$|\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{U}(t)\,|\psi(0)\rangle, \qquad
  \boxed{\hat{U}(t) = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}}.$$

$\hat{U}(t)$ is the **time-evolution operator** (propagator): it carries the initial state forward
to time $t$.

### Why the exponential is a gate

1. **It is unitary.** Since $\hat{H} = \hat{H}^\dagger$ (Hermitian), $e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}$ satisfies
   $\hat{U}^\dagger\hat{U} = I$. Unitarity is exactly what a quantum gate must have: it preserves
   the norm (total probability stays 1) and is reversible.
2. **Gates are unitaries.** In the circuit model a gate *is* a unitary operator on the qubits.
   So "the corresponding unitary gate is time evolution" means the gate is physically realized by
   letting the system evolve under its Hamiltonian for a time $t$.

So the relationship is: $\hat{H}$ (Hermitian) is the energy observable — its eigenvalues are the
measurable energies and its eigenstates are the energy basis — while $\hat{U}(t) = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}$
(unitary) is the transform that *evolves* a state in time, driven by that energy.

### Reading the operator exponential concretely

$e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}$ is a matrix exponential, $e^{A} = \sum_n A^n/n!$. Evaluate it in the energy
eigenbasis: if $\hat{H}|E_n\rangle = E_n|E_n\rangle$, then

$$\hat{U}(t)\,|E_n\rangle = e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}\,|E_n\rangle.$$

Each energy eigenstate just **acquires a phase** $e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}$ rotating at a rate set by its
energy (higher energy, faster phase). A superposition evolves by having its components spin at
different rates; the *relative* phase between them drives observable interference.

### Tie-back to the project (Bloch animations)

For the standard qubit Hamiltonian $\hat{H} = \tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}\hat{Z}$,

$$\hat{U}(t) = e^{-i\omega t\,\hat{Z}/2}
            = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\omega t/2} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{+i\omega t/2} \end{pmatrix}
            = R_z(\omega t),$$

a standard $Z$-rotation gate. On the Bloch sphere it is steady precession about the $z$-axis at
angular frequency $\omega$. The phase separations and "global phase offset" keyframes animated in
`C_to_Bloch.py` are exactly this: phases accruing at rates set by the diagonal energies of
$\hat{H}$.

**Bottom line:** a quantum gate is "run the Hamiltonian for a while." Choose $\hat{H}$ and a
duration $t$, and $e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}$ is the resulting gate. Conversely, any desired gate can
be engineered by arranging the right Hamiltonian for the right time — which is how real hardware
implements gates, via timed control pulses.


## Observables and how to measure them

(Builds on **Measurement: spectral decomposition, the Born rule, and projection**
above, which established how a Hermitian observable yields eigenvalue outcomes and
projector-based probabilities. This section catalogs the specific qubit observables
and the hardware recipes for measuring each.)

Energy is just one observable among infinitely many. Any Hermitian operator on the
qubit's Hilbert space is a legitimate observable; what makes an operator measurable is
Hermiticity (real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenstates), not any special status of energy.

For a single qubit the natural observables are the three Pauli operators, each
with eigenvalues $\pm 1$ and its own eigenbasis:

- $\sigma_z$ measures in the $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ basis (the
  computational / energy basis). This is the "which level" measurement.
- $\sigma_x$ measures in the $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$ basis.
- $\sigma_y$ measures in the $\{|{+i}\rangle, |{-i}\rangle\}$ basis.

These correspond to the three axes of the Bloch sphere ($z$, $x$, $y$). More
generally, measuring along an arbitrary axis $\hat{n} = (n_x, n_y, n_z)$ uses
the observable

$$\hat{n} \cdot \vec{\sigma} = n_x \sigma_x + n_y \sigma_y + n_z \sigma_z,$$

which is Hermitian for any real unit vector $\hat{n}$, giving a continuum of
possible measurements.

The energy observable $\hat{H} \propto \sigma_z$ for a two-level system, so
"measuring energy" and "measuring $\sigma_z$" are the same measurement up to
scaling and offset. Energy is special physically (it drives time evolution),
but it is not special as a measurement; it is just the $z$-axis choice.

### Hardware measures only $\sigma_z$

Real hardware usually only performs a $\sigma_z$ (computational-basis) measurement
directly. To measure $\sigma_x$ or $\sigma_y$ you first apply a basis-change gate that
rotates the desired axis onto $z$, then do the ordinary $\sigma_z$ measurement and
reinterpret the bits. The per-axis recipe:

- $\sigma_z$: measure directly (no pre-gate).
- $\sigma_x$: apply $H$, then measure in $Z$.
- $\sigma_y$: apply $S^\dagger$ then $H$, then measure in $Z$.

The gate conventions:

$$S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
S^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Why the $\sigma_y$ recipe works: $\sigma_y$ has eigenstates $|{+i}\rangle, |{-i}\rangle$ on the
Bloch $y$-axis. $S^\dagger$ rotates the Bloch sphere by $-90^\circ$ about $z$,
carrying the $y$-axis onto the $x$-axis; $H$ then swaps the $x$- and $z$-axes,
carrying it onto $z$. After the pair, the state that was a $\sigma_y$ eigenstate
is now a $\sigma_z$ eigenstate, so the computational-basis readout reports it:

- outcome $0 \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma_y = +1$ (state was $|{+i}\rangle$)
- outcome $1 \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma_y = -1$ (state was $|{-i}\rangle$)

In Qiskit the $\sigma_y$ case is `qc.sdg(q); qc.h(q); qc.measure(q, c)`. The
gate always runs *before* the measurement, since measurement is the last,
irreversible step.


## Density matrix, energy eigenstates, and decoherence


### The density matrix

For a pure state $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$, the density matrix is
the outer product:

$$\rho = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi| = \begin{pmatrix} |\alpha|^2 & \alpha\beta^* \\ \alpha^*\beta & |\beta|^2 \end{pmatrix}$$

The diagonal elements ($|\alpha|^2$, $|\beta|^2$) are the measurement probabilities ---
they tell you "how much $|0\rangle$" and "how much $|1\rangle$." The off-diagonal elements
($\alpha\beta^*$ and its conjugate) encode the *coherence* --- the phase relationship
between the two components. They are what make interference possible. If the off-diagonals
are zero, you have a *classical mixture* (the qubit is in $|0\rangle$ OR $|1\rangle$ with
some probability, but not in superposition). If they are nonzero, you have genuine quantum
superposition and interference can occur.

The density matrix is necessary (beyond the state vector) when you have incomplete
knowledge --- either a statistical ensemble of states, or a qubit entangled with an
environment you cannot access.


### Energy of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$

For a physical qubit, $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are energy eigenstates with distinct
energies. In IBM's transmon qubits (superconducting circuits), the energy splitting is
engineered to be $E_1 - E_0 = h\nu$ where $\nu \approx 5$ GHz, giving a gap of roughly
$2 \times 10^{-24}$ J ($\sim 20$ $\mu$eV). This is why they operate at 15 millikelvin ---
thermal energy $k_BT$ must be far below the gap to prevent spontaneous excitation.

A qubit is correctly understood as a proxy for *any* two-state quantum system. The
abstraction is universal. But every physical *implementation* of that abstraction has a
specific energy structure:

| Implementation | $|0\rangle$ | $|1\rangle$ | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transmon (IBM) | ground state of LC oscillator | first excited state | $\sim 5$ GHz |
| Trapped ion | ground-state hyperfine level | excited hyperfine level | $\sim 1$ GHz |
| Photon polarization | horizontal | vertical | 0 (degenerate) |
| Spin-1/2 in B field | spin-down (lower) | spin-up (higher) | $\gamma B$ |
| Mach-Zehnder interferometer | detector 0 clicks | detector 1 clicks | 0 (path encoding) |
| Double-slit (per position) | photon arrives | photon does not arrive | 0 (path encoding) |

The energy gap is what makes $T_1$ decay directional: the qubit preferentially relaxes
*from* $|1\rangle$ *to* $|0\rangle$ (emitting a photon into the environment), not the
reverse. This is exactly like a two-level atom decaying from its excited state.


### Decoherence on the Bloch sphere

- **$T_1$ (energy relaxation):** The Bloch vector shrinks *toward the north pole*
  $(0, 0, 1)$. The state drifts toward $|0\rangle$ regardless of where it started.
  Geometrically: the vector's $z$-component increases toward $+1$ while its length
  contracts.
- **$T_2$ (dephasing):** The Bloch vector shrinks *toward the $z$-axis*. The $x$ and $y$
  components decay while $z$ is preserved. The state loses its azimuthal angle (phase)
  but retains its measurement probabilities. Geometrically: the point stays at the same
  latitude but spirals inward to the axis.
- **Full decoherence ($T_1 + T_2$):** The Bloch vector collapses to a point near
  $(0, 0, 1)$ --- a classical mixture biased toward $|0\rangle$.


### Decoherence on the $\mathbb{C}^2$ double-disk diagram

- **$T_2$ dephasing:** The second disk's dot (the $\beta$ coefficient) *rotates randomly*
  --- its angle becomes uncertain while its magnitude stays fixed. One would draw it as a
  smeared arc rather than a point. The first disk's dot is unaffected.
- **$T_1$ relaxation:** The second disk's dot shrinks toward the origin (losing magnitude)
  while the first disk's dot grows toward $1 + 0i$. Probability flows from $|1\rangle$ to
  $|0\rangle$.
- **Full decoherence:** First disk has a dot at $1 + 0i$, second disk has a dot at the
  origin --- the state $|0\rangle$, with no phase information surviving.


### The interior of the Bloch sphere

The key point: decoherence does not move you to a random pure state on the Bloch sphere
surface. It moves you *inside* the sphere (a mixed state). Points on the surface are pure
states; points inside are classical mixtures. The density matrix formalism is needed
precisely to describe these interior points, which the state vector $|\psi\rangle$ cannot
represent.


## Why is the dimension of the simplest Hilbert space 2?

Strictly speaking, the simplest Hilbert space is $\mathbb{C}^1$ --- one-dimensional. But
it is trivial: there is only one state (up to global phase), no superposition, no
measurement uncertainty, no dynamics. A single complex number with $|\alpha|^2 = 1$ is
just a phase. Nothing happens.

The interesting minimum is $\mathbb{C}^2$ because dimension 2 is the smallest where:

- **Superposition exists.** You need at least two basis vectors to form a nontrivial
  linear combination $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$.
- **Measurement is non-trivial.** With two orthogonal outcomes, there is genuine
  uncertainty --- probability splits between them.
- **Non-commuting observables appear.** In $\mathbb{C}^1$ every operator is a scalar
  (everything commutes). In $\mathbb{C}^2$ you get $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y$, $\sigma_z$
  which do not commute --- giving complementarity, uncertainty relations, and incompatible
  measurements.
- **Unitary evolution is non-trivial.** In $\mathbb{C}^1$, all unitaries are just phase
  rotations ($e^{i\theta}$). In $\mathbb{C}^2$ you get SU(2) --- a continuous group of
  rotations on the Bloch sphere.

So "dimension 2" is not a postulate --- it is the minimum where quantum mechanics actually
*does* anything. The physical reason it is realized in nature: many systems have exactly
two accessible states (spin-$\frac{1}{2}$, photon polarization, two energy levels of an
atom). These are genuine two-state systems, not truncations of something larger.


## What is a quantum system?

A quantum system is a set of degrees of freedom, isolated from everything else, whose
state is a unit vector in a Hilbert space and whose evolution is unitary.

Three operative parts:

1. **Isolated** --- we draw a boundary and declare everything inside to be the system,
   everything outside to not exist. This is what makes a pure state description valid.
2. **State is a vector** --- not a point in phase space (classical) but a superposition of
   basis states, with complex amplitudes.
3. **Evolution is unitary** --- the system evolves by rotations in Hilbert space,
   preserving probability, until we break the isolation (measurement / decoherence).

A proposed informal definition --- "a microscopic assembly studied in a model universe, as
if there was nothing else" --- captures the isolation requirement. What it leaves implicit
is the *structure* of the state space. A classical isolated system is also "a thing studied
as if nothing else existed," but its state is a point in phase space, not a vector in a
complex inner product space. The Hilbert space structure (superposition, complex
amplitudes, interference) is what makes it quantum.

The word "microscopic" is slightly misleading --- quantum systems can be macroscopic
(superconducting circuits, Bose-Einstein condensates). What matters is isolation from
decoherence, not physical size.


## Quantum vector vs classical point: why both are n numbers but fundamentally different

Both a classical state and a quantum state can be written as a list of $n$ numbers. A
classical state of two particles in 3D is 12 numbers (positions and momenta); a qubit
state is 2 complex numbers. Both are coordinates. The structural difference is what the
coordinates mean and what operations respect that meaning.

### Classical: a point in phase space

A classical state $(q_1, q_2, \ldots, p_1, p_2, \ldots)$ is a point in phase space. The
coordinates are *directly observable quantities* --- each one is independently measurable.
You can know $q_1$ without disturbing $q_2$. The space is a manifold: the relevant
structure is topology and smoothness (trajectories are curves). There is no algebraic
relationship between different points --- the state at position A and the state at position
B do not combine in any meaningful sense.

### Quantum: a vector in Hilbert space

A quantum state $(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots, \alpha_n)$ is a vector. The coordinates are
*amplitudes for mutually exclusive outcomes*. They are not independently accessible --- you
get exactly one outcome upon measurement, sampled from the distribution $|\alpha_k|^2$.
The crucial structures that have no classical analog:

1. **Superposition is physically meaningful.** If $|\psi\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle$ are
   states, then $c_1|\psi\rangle + c_2|\phi\rangle$ is also a state --- and it is *not*
   "the system is in $|\psi\rangle$ or $|\phi\rangle$ with some probability." It is a
   genuinely new state with interference effects. Classically, the midpoint between two
   phase space points is just another unrelated point --- there is no sense in which it
   "superposes" the two original states.

2. **The inner product is physical.** $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ gives the transition
   amplitude between states. Orthogonal states ($\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = 0$) are
   perfectly distinguishable. This has no classical analog --- the distance between two
   classical phase space points has no probabilistic interpretation.

3. **Bases are choices, not facts.** The same quantum state looks different in different
   bases (Z-basis vs X-basis), and *both are equally real descriptions of the same state*.
   Classically, $(q, p)$ coordinates are the state --- there is no alternative
   "complementary" description where position becomes undefined.

4. **Components interfere.** Because amplitudes are complex,
   $|\alpha + \beta|^2 \neq |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2$. The cross terms
   $\alpha^*\beta + \alpha\beta^*$ are interference. Classically, probabilities just add
   --- there are no cross terms.

### The punchline

A classical point is a *label* --- it says "the system is here." A quantum vector is a
*recipe for probability with phase* --- it says "if you measure in this basis, here is the
amplitude for each outcome, and those amplitudes interfere before you square them." Both
are lists of numbers, but the classical list has no algebraic structure connecting its
entries, while the quantum list lives in a vector space where addition, scaling, and inner
products all have physical meaning.


## The $|1\rangle$ outcome represents the darkness of the fringe

The circuit maps interference intensity to $P(|0\rangle) = \cos^2(\varphi/2)$. 
The complement
$P(|1\rangle) = \sin^2(\varphi/2)$ does not represent "photon went elsewhere" --- it
represents the *dark* part of the fringe at this position. The $|1\rangle$ shots are
complementary statistical draws with no direct physical meaning in the "where does the photon land" picture.

In the physical experiment, a photon hits the screen 
*somewhere* --- it always lands. The probability distributes across 
all positions and sums to 1. The circuit model does not
simulate a single photon's full journey. It computes the probability 
density function pointwise. Each circuit instance answers an independent 
binary question; the full-screen picture emerges only when results are 
assembled and normalized.


## Is the particle "headed for" a particular detector?

Place a detector at the sweep angle corresponding to relative phase
$\varphi = \nu - \eta$ (the phase of $\beta$ minus the phase of $\alpha$). The circuit
returns $P(|0\rangle) = \cos^2(\varphi/2)$, the probability of a click *at that angle*. A
tempting reading is: "the particle is headed for this detector, and by virtue of
interference it either is or is not detected." Taken literally this is **not accurate** ---
it reintroduces exactly the classical intuition the double slit is meant to destroy.

**Why it misleads:**

- *"Headed for this detector"* presumes the particle already has a definite direction or
  destination in flight --- a which-path-like property. If it did, interference would
  vanish (the which-path / quantum-eraser lesson). In flight the particle has no definite
  path and no definite destination; it is a superposition over both slit-paths to every
  position at once.
- *"Interference either lets it be detected or not"* frames interference as a yes/no gate
  applied to a particle already en route. What interference actually does is fix the
  complex **amplitude** for arrival at that position. Probability is $|\text{amplitude}|^2$.
  It is not that a particle "was coming and was turned away"; it is that the amplitude to
  arrive there is large (bright fringe, the two path-amplitudes add in phase) or near zero
  (dark fringe, they cancel).

**The honest restatement:** a particle leaves the source in a superposition of both
slit-paths with no destination. For the detector at angle $\varphi$, QM assigns the
coherent sum of the two path-amplitudes, whose relative phase is $\varphi$; interference
is the addition of amplitudes *before* squaring; the click probability is
$|\text{sum}|^2 = \cos^2(\varphi/2)$.

**Circuit-specific caveat:** the single-qubit H-P-H circuit models **one detector position
in isolation** --- a binary "click here, yes or no?" --- not a particle choosing among many
detectors. The full fringe pattern is assembled by sweeping $\varphi$ across many separate
circuits and normalizing afterward; it is not one particle's journey across a screen. So
the $|1\rangle$ complement is the dark-fringe weight at *this* position, not "the particle
went to a different angle" (see "The $|1\rangle$ outcome represents the darkness of the
fringe" above).

**Verdict:**

- "Measuring the probability of detection at that sweep angle" --- accurate.
- "The particle is headed for this particular detector" --- not accurate; there is no
  pre-existing destination.
- "By virtue of interference it either is or is not detected" --- acceptable only if read
  as "interference sets the arrival amplitude, hence the click probability"; misleading if
  read as a gate acting on a particle already en route.


## Digression on $\chi^2$ noise in the DSE

Interpreting the double-slit $\chi^2$ chart: the distribution shows large $\chi^2$ maxima
at each fringe minimum and smaller $\chi^2$ peaks at the fringe maxima. (We ignore here
any post-hoc $\mathrm{sinc}^2$ envelope.) If the noise were evenly distributed across all
bins, uniform noise would produce a larger $\chi^2$ where the theoretical count is near
zero --- but why are there also secondary spikes at the fringe maxima, and why are the
dark-fringe peaks so much larger than the bright-fringe ones? The analysis below resolves
both questions.


### Standard deviation at screen position $i$

At a given screen position $i$, the theoretical probability of measuring $|0\rangle$ is
some definite value --- say $p_i = 0.62$. Each shot is a binary trial: either $|0\rangle$
(success, probability $p_i$) or $|1\rangle$ (probability $1 - p_i$). Over $N$ shots the
count of $|0\rangle$ outcomes follows a binomial distribution with standard deviation:

$$\sigma_i = \sqrt{N \cdot p_i \cdot (1 - p_i)}$$

This $\sigma_i$ is the spread you expect from dice-rolling alone --- no hardware errors,
just the irreducible randomness of sampling a probability. It depends on two things: $N$
(how many shots) and $p_i$ (where on the fringe pattern you are).

For $p_i = 0.62$:
- $N = 10$ shots: $\sigma = \sqrt{10 \times 0.62 \times 0.38} = \sqrt{2.36} \approx 1.5$ counts
- $N = 100$ shots: $\sigma = \sqrt{100 \times 0.62 \times 0.38} = \sqrt{23.6} \approx 4.9$ counts
- $N = 1000$ shots: $\sigma = \sqrt{1000 \times 0.62 \times 0.38} = \sqrt{236} \approx 15.4$ counts

The absolute spread grows as $\sqrt{N}$, but the *relative* uncertainty (as a fraction of
$N$) shrinks as $1/\sqrt{N}$. With 10 shots, your estimate of $p$ fluctuates by $\pm 15\%$;
with 1000 shots, by $\pm 1.5\%$. More shots give a sharper estimate, but with diminishing
returns --- you need 100 times more shots to improve precision by a factor of 10.


### What $\chi^2$ measures

Chi-squared is a normalized error metric. At each screen position $i$, we have three
quantities: the observed count $O_i$, the expected (theoretical) count $E_i$, and the
standard deviation $\sigma_i$ expected from pure sampling noise. The per-bin contribution
is:

$$(O_i - E_i)^2 \;/\; \sigma_i^2$$

This asks: "how many standard deviations away from theory was this bin, squared?" A value
near 1 means the deviation is consistent with dice-rolling. A value much greater than 1
means something beyond dice-rolling is going on.

The quantities:
- $O_i$ = observed count of $|0\rangle$ outcomes out of $N$ shots at position $i$
- $E_i = N \cdot \cos^2(\phi_i / 2)$ = theoretical count from the H-P-H interference formula
- $\sigma_i = \sqrt{N \cdot p_i(1 - p_i)}$ where $p_i = E_i / N$ = binomial standard deviation

The total $\chi^2 = \sum_i (O_i - E_i)^2 / \sigma_i^2$, divided by degrees of freedom
(number of bins minus 1), should be near 1 for a perfect device with only sampling noise.


### Why $\sigma_i$ is symmetric in $p$ and $(1-p)$

The binomial variance $Np(1-p)$ is maximized at $p = 0.5$ and minimized symmetrically at
both extremes. Whether you are nearly always measuring $|0\rangle$ or nearly always
measuring $|1\rangle$, the spread of the count is equally narrow. This is the variance of
a coin flip: a heavily biased coin has low variance regardless of which side is favored.
Counting $|0\rangle$ outcomes at probability $p$ is statistically identical to counting
$|1\rangle$ outcomes at probability $(1-p)$.

A consequence of this symmetry: a fixed absolute readout error produces roughly the same
absolute shift in counts everywhere, but at *both* the bright peaks and the dark troughs
the variance denominator shrinks, amplifying the $\chi^2$ contribution. So one expects
secondary spikes at the bright fringes as well as the large spikes at the dark fringes ---
and if readout error were symmetric, both extremes would produce *identical* $\chi^2$
contributions. The next section shows this symmetric expectation and where it breaks down.


### Symmetric readout error predicts equal contributions at both extremes

Assume $N = 2000$ shots and a symmetric 4\% readout error rate (each shot has a 4\% chance
of flipping the outcome).

**Example 1 --- dark fringe center** ($p_i = 0.01$, theory expects $E_i = 20$ counts of $|0\rangle$):

- $E_i = 2000 \times 0.01 = 20$.
- Readout error flips 4\% of the 1980 shots that should have been $|1\rangle$ into $|0\rangle$: $0.04 \times 1980 = 79$ extra $|0\rangle$ counts.
- Also flips 4\% of the 20 true $|0\rangle$ into $|1\rangle$: lose $0.04 \times 20 = 0.8$ counts.
- Net: $O_i \approx 20 + 79 - 1 = 98$.
- Variance: $\sigma^2 = N \cdot p_i(1-p_i) = 2000 \times 0.01 \times 0.99 = 19.8$.
- Chi-squared contribution: $(98 - 20)^2 / 19.8 = 6084 / 19.8 = \mathbf{307}$.

**Example 2 --- bright fringe center** ($p_i = 0.99$, theory expects $E_i = 1980$ counts of $|0\rangle$):

- $E_i = 2000 \times 0.99 = 1980$.
- Readout error flips 4\% of the 1980 true $|0\rangle$ into $|1\rangle$: lose $0.04 \times 1980 = 79$ counts.
- Also flips 4\% of the 20 true $|1\rangle$ into $|0\rangle$: gain $0.04 \times 20 = 0.8$ counts.
- Net: $O_i \approx 1980 - 79 + 1 = 1902$.
- Variance: $\sigma^2 = N \cdot p_i(1-p_i) = 2000 \times 0.99 \times 0.01 = 19.8$.
- Chi-squared contribution: $(1902 - 1980)^2 / 19.8 = 6084 / 19.8 = \mathbf{307}$.

Identical chi-squared contributions --- the formula is perfectly symmetric in $p$ and
$(1-p)$. The same readout error rate produces the same number of flipped counts at both
extremes, and the same tiny variance. But the symmetric model cannot explain a $\sim 25$
times asymmetry between dark and bright fringes observed on real hardware. That asymmetry
has a physical origin.


### Resolution: asymmetric readout error from T1 decay

IBM transmon qubits have directionally biased readout error. During the $\sim 1\;\mu$s
measurement pulse, the qubit can spontaneously relax from $|1\rangle$ to $|0\rangle$ (T1
decay), but spontaneous excitation from $|0\rangle$ to $|1\rangle$ is thermally suppressed
at 15 mK. Typical rates:

- P(read 0 $|$ true state 1) $\approx$ 3--5\%
- P(read 1 $|$ true state 0) $\approx$ 0.5--1\%

Worked examples with $N = 2000$ shots:

**Dark fringe** ($p_i = 0.01$, theory expects $E_i = 20$ counts of $|0\rangle$, almost all shots truly $|1\rangle$):
- 1980 true $|1\rangle$ shots misread as $|0\rangle$ at 4\%: gain $\sim 79$ false counts
- 20 true $|0\rangle$ shots misread as $|1\rangle$ at 0.5\%: lose $\sim 0.1$ counts
- Net observed: $O_i \approx 99$. Error = +79 counts.
- $\sigma^2 = 2000 \times 0.01 \times 0.99 = 19.8$
- Chi-squared contribution: $79^2 / 19.8 \approx 315$

**Bright fringe** ($p_i = 0.99$, theory expects $E_i = 1980$ counts of $|0\rangle$, almost all shots truly $|0\rangle$):
- 1980 true $|0\rangle$ shots misread as $|1\rangle$ at 0.5\%: lose $\sim 10$ counts
- 20 true $|1\rangle$ shots misread as $|0\rangle$ at 4\%: gain $\sim 0.8$ counts
- Net observed: $O_i \approx 1971$. Error = $-9$ counts.
- $\sigma^2 = 2000 \times 0.99 \times 0.01 = 19.8$
- Chi-squared contribution: $9^2 / 19.8 \approx 4$

The asymmetry ratio is $79^2 / 9^2 \approx 77$, consistent with the observed $\sim 25$
times difference (real error rates are somewhat less extreme than 4\% vs 0.5\%). The
$p(1-p)$ in the variance comes from the binomial distribution --- the variance of a coin
flip, not an assumption about the hardware. The statistical benchmark ($\sigma$) is
symmetric; the *hardware* error (T1 decay biasing toward $|0\rangle$) is what breaks the
symmetry.


### Why the simulator shows no such pattern

The AerSimulator's coin flip is perfectly symmetric --- no directional bias. Both fringe
extremes contribute chi-squared values near 1 (pure sampling noise). The simulator
confirms that the asymmetric pattern is a hardware signature, not a statistical artifact.


## Why a symmetric (Hermitian) matrix has real eigenvalues

Let A be Hermitian (meaning A equals its own conjugate transpose) and suppose 
$Av = \lambda v$ for eigenvector $v$ and eigenvalue $\lambda$ (both possibly complex).


### Preliminary: the dagger operation on vectors

For a column vector $v$ with complex entries, the **dagger** $v^\dagger$ means: transpose
the column into a row, then complex-conjugate every entry. If
$v = (v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n)^T$ then $v^\dagger = (v_1^*, v_2^*, \ldots, v_n^*)$ as a
row vector. This is the conjugate transpose.

For a matrix $A$, the dagger $A^\dagger$ means: transpose and conjugate every entry.
A matrix is Hermitian when $A^\dagger = A$.


### Preliminary: why $Av = \lambda v$ implies $v^\dagger A^\dagger = \lambda^* v^\dagger$

Start with:

$$Av = \lambda v$$

Take the transpose of both sides. Recall that for any product, $(XY)^T = Y^T X^T$:

$$(Av)^T = (\lambda v)^T$$

$$v^T A^T = \lambda \, v^T$$

Now take the complex conjugate of every entry (denoted by an overbar). The conjugate of
a product distributes:

$$\overline{v^T} \; \overline{A^T} = \overline{\lambda} \; \overline{v^T}$$

But transpose-then-conjugate is the dagger, so $\overline{v^T} = v^\dagger$ and
$\overline{A^T} = A^\dagger$ and $\overline{\lambda} = \lambda^*$. Therefore:

$$v^\dagger A^\dagger = \lambda^* v^\dagger$$

This is just the statement: "conjugate-transpose both sides of the eigenvalue equation."


### The proof

Since A is Hermitian, replace $A^\dagger$ with A:

$$v^\dagger A = \lambda^* v^\dagger$$

Multiply on the right by $v$:

$$v^\dagger A v = \lambda^* (v^\dagger v)$$

But from the eigenvalue equation, multiply $Av = \lambda v$ on the left by $v^\dagger$:

$$v^\dagger A v = \lambda (v^\dagger v)$$

Equate the two expressions and divide by the positive quantity $v^\dagger v$ (which is
the squared norm of $v$, strictly positive for any nonzero vector):

$$\lambda^* = \lambda$$

A number equal to its own complex conjugate is real. The same argument applies to real
symmetric matrices (real entries plus symmetric implies Hermitian). This is why quantum
mechanics requires observables to be Hermitian: eigenvalues are measurement outcomes, and
measurements yield real numbers.


## Multi-qubit systems: tensor product, entanglement, and CHSH

### The qubit as the atomic unit

In the current landscape of quantum computing, the qubit (a two-level
quantum system, Hilbert space $\mathbb{C}^2$) is the universal atomic unit. All major 
platforms — IBM, Google, IonQ, Quantinuum, Rigetti, QuEra — are built on qubits.

There is research into **qutrits** ($\mathbb{C}^3$, three-level systems) and more generally 
**qudits** ($\mathbb{C}^d$, d-level systems), but these are not commercially available and 
remain a niche research direction. For practical purposes: qubit = atom of quantum 
computation.


### The tensor product $\otimes$

Once you understand a single qubit (state in $\mathbb{C}^2$, Bloch sphere, gates as
rotations), multi-qubit systems are built via the **tensor product**. The tensor product
combines two Hilbert spaces $\mathcal{H}_A$ (dimension $m$) and $\mathcal{H}_B$
(dimension $n$) into a joint space $\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$ of dimension
$mn$ -- the *product*, not the sum. If $\{|i\rangle\}$ is a basis of $\mathcal{H}_A$ and
$\{|j\rangle\}$ a basis of $\mathcal{H}_B$, then $\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$ is
spanned by all pairs $|i\rangle \otimes |j\rangle$ (often written $|i\rangle|j\rangle$ or
$|ij\rangle$).

For qubits the dimension doubles with each added qubit:

- 1 qubit: Hilbert space is $\mathbb{C}^2$ (dimension 2)
- 2 qubits: $\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 = \mathbb{C}^4$ (dimension 4)
- 3 qubits: $\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 = \mathbb{C}^8$ (dimension 8)
- n qubits: $(\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes n} = \mathbb{C}^{2^n}$ (dimension $2^n$)

The state of an n-qubit system is a unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^{2^n}$. The basis states 
are all $2^n$ binary strings: for 2 qubits, $\{|00\rangle, |01\rangle, |10\rangle, |11\rangle\}$.
A general 2-qubit state is

$$|\psi\rangle = \alpha_{00}|00\rangle + \alpha_{01}|01\rangle + \alpha_{10}|10\rangle + \alpha_{11}|11\rangle$$

with $\sum |\alpha_{ij}|^2 = 1$.

#### A note on notation: $|00\rangle$ vs $|0\rangle|0\rangle$ vs $|0\rangle \otimes |0\rangle$

These three forms denote the **identical vector**. The differences are purely
notational, with no interpretational content:

$$|0\rangle \otimes |0\rangle \;=\; |0\rangle|0\rangle \;=\; |00\rangle$$

- $|0\rangle \otimes |0\rangle$ names the operation explicitly: the tensor (Kronecker)
  product that combines the two single-qubit spaces into the joint space
  $\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 = \mathbb{C}^4$.
- $|0\rangle|0\rangle$ drops the $\otimes$ symbol. Juxtaposition of kets *means* tensor
  product by convention --- pure shorthand.
- $|00\rangle$ concatenates the labels inside a single ket. The left digit is the first
  qubit, the right digit the second (an ordering convention one fixes and keeps). Still
  the same vector.

So $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$ and
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle|0\rangle + |1\rangle|1\rangle)$ are the same state written
two ways.

**The one caveat.** The equivalence is notational, not a claim about separability.
Writing $|00\rangle$ does *not* assert that the state is a product state --- it happens
that $|00\rangle$ factors, but the concatenated form is used freely for entangled states
too, as in $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$. The distinction to keep: an
$\otimes$ placed *between two kets* asserts that those two factors are separable;
concatenation *inside one ket* asserts nothing about entanglement. That is the only place
the notations carry different information, and it concerns the whole expression, not the
individual basis ket.

Concretely, on vectors and operators the tensor product is the Kronecker product, which
multiplies every component of the first by the whole second:

$$\begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix} \otimes \begin{pmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} a_1 b_1 \\ a_1 b_2 \\ a_2 b_1 \\ a_2 b_2 \end{pmatrix}.$$


### Entanglement

The single-qubit Bloch sphere picture does **not** straightforwardly generalize to 
multiple qubits. A 2-qubit state lives in $\mathbb{C}^4$, which is not representable as 
two independent Bloch spheres. The reason: **entanglement**.

A state like $|00\rangle$ is a **product (separable) state** — it can be written as 
$|0\rangle \otimes |0\rangle$. Each qubit has its own independent state.

But the Bell state $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$ is **entangled** — it 
cannot be factored into a product $|\phi\rangle_A \otimes |\chi\rangle_B$ of individual
qubit states. The qubits do not have independent descriptions. That the joint space is
bigger than the set of product states is precisely what makes room for entanglement --- and
it is what makes quantum computing powerful (and the state space grow exponentially).

So: the single qubit is the atom, the tensor product is how atoms combine, and 
entanglement is the qualitatively new phenomenon that emerges when they do.

#### Is "entangled or not" ever ambiguous?

For a fully specified *pure* state vector, no. Entanglement is a definite yes/no property
of the vector: the state is entangled iff it *cannot* be written as a single tensor
product $|\psi\rangle_A \otimes |\phi\rangle_B$. One applies the factorization test (or the
amplitude-matrix determinant heuristic — see Q4.md "Reading states: how to tell entangled
from not") and gets a definite answer. For example $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle +
|01\rangle) = |0\rangle|+\rangle$ factors (product state), while
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$ provably does not (entangled). No judgment
call is involved.

There are two places where "more context" genuinely does matter, though neither applies to
simple two-qubit examples in the standard basis:

- **Entanglement is relative to the choice of subsystems.** "Entangled" is defined with
  respect to a particular split of the system into parts — which degrees of freedom are
  "A" and which are "B". For a fixed, standard qubit decomposition the answer is
  unambiguous, but regroup or redefine the tensor factors and the same global vector can be
  product with respect to one decomposition and entangled with respect to another.
  Entanglement is a property of a state *together with* a chosen factorization, not of the
  bare vector alone.
- **Pure vs. mixed states.** The clean "does it factor?" test is for pure states (single
  state vectors). For mixed states (density matrices) the definition is subtler — a mixed
  state is separable iff it can be written as a probabilistic mixture
  $\rho = \sum_i p_i\, \rho_i^A \otimes \rho_i^B$ of product states — and deciding
  separability is genuinely hard in general. (See Q4.md on reduced density matrices and
  mixed states.)

So: pin down the state vector and the subsystem split, and entanglement is never a matter
of opinion.


### Does the double-slit experiment really carry "all" of QM, if entanglement goes beyond it?

**The apparent tension.** Feynman (Lectures, Vol. III) says the double-slit experiment
"has in it the heart of quantum mechanics" and "contains the only mystery." Yet
Schrodinger called entanglement "*the* characteristic trait of quantum mechanics, the one
that enforces its entire departure from classical lines of thought." Two authorities, two
"the essential thing" claims, apparently pointing at different phenomena -- and
entanglement seems to live entirely outside the single-particle DSE.

The resolution: they identify the mystery at two different levels of structure, and both
are right about their own level.

**What the DSE fully captures.** Feynman's "only mystery" is the **superposition principle
plus the Born rule**: a single system can be a coherent sum of alternatives, *amplitudes*
add (not probabilities), and $|\cdot|^2$ converts amplitude to probability. The DSE
displays all of this -- superposition (sum over paths), interference of amplitudes (the
fringes), the measurement/collapse puzzle (why which-path erases the fringes), and the
impossibility of a classical which-path story. Everything that makes a *single* quantum
system strange is present. The project's own H-P-H circuit makes the point concrete: it
reproduces the full DSE on **one qubit** -- a single $\mathbb{C}^2$ system, no second
party, no entanglement.

**What entanglement adds, and why the DSE cannot reach it.** The precise reason
entanglement goes beyond the DSE: **entanglement is a property of *composite* systems --
it lives in the tensor-product structure of two or more subsystems.** The DSE is a
single-particle phenomenon; its state space is a single factor. Entanglement requires at
least two subsystems whose joint state $|\psi\rangle_{AB}$ cannot be factored as
$|\phi\rangle_A \otimes |\chi\rangle_B$. No single-particle experiment can show it, because
there is no $\otimes$ to break. So the genuinely new mystery is not superposition (the DSE
already has that) but:

- **Nonlocal correlations.** Measuring $A$ instantly fixes the description of a distant
  $B$, with correlations (Bell / CHSH) stronger than any local-hidden-variable theory
  permits. This has no single-particle analog.
- **The relativity of superposition.** An entangled pair has a definite *joint* pure state
  while each party alone is in a mixed state with no pure state of its own. "Superposition
  of what?" becomes factorization-dependent -- a question the DSE cannot even pose.

**Holding both claims at once.** Two layers of departure from classical physics:

1. **One system, many alternatives $\to$ superposition + Born rule.** Feynman's "only
   mystery," fully contained in the DSE. The departure from classical *states*.
2. **Many systems, one joint state $\to$ entanglement + nonlocality.** Schrodinger's
   "characteristic trait," requiring the tensor product and invisible to the DSE. The
   departure from classical *composition* -- the classical assumption that the whole is
   just the parts plus their local properties.

Entanglement does not contradict Feynman: an entangled state is itself a superposition in
the joint space, e.g. $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$. Superposition is the
atomic mystery, and the DSE carries it. Entanglement is superposition *applied to composite
systems*, unlocking a strictly stronger phenomenon no single-particle setup can display.

**In one line:** the DSE exhausts the mystery of a single ray in Hilbert space;
entanglement is the mystery of how Hilbert spaces combine. Feynman described the first,
Schrodinger the second -- each "the essential thing" at its own level.


### Bell's inequality and CHSH

CHSH (Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt, 1969) is the experimentally practical form of Bell's
inequality. Two separated parties each choose one of two measurement settings and record
$\pm 1$ outcomes; from the correlations one forms the quantity

$$S = E(a,b) - E(a,b') + E(a',b) + E(a',b'),$$

where $E(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the correlation (expectation of the product of outcomes) for a
given pair of settings. Any **local hidden-variable** theory obeys

$$|S| \le 2,$$

whereas an entangled quantum state can reach the Tsirelson bound
$|S| = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$. Measuring $|S| > 2$ in the lab falsifies local realism.

See `Q4.md` "Entanglement Refutation of Deterministic Interpretation" for the full
treatment of Bell/CHSH, including the experiment, the $2\sqrt{2}$ violation, and the
project's two-qubit demonstration reproducing it on a quantum computer.


## Measurement and two-qubit entanglement are the same process

A natural puzzle: Schrödinger's cat describes an "observation" as a kind of entanglement
that *destroys* coherence, yet the entanglement in a clean two-qubit pair (the resource
behind a Bell/CHSH test) does *not* destroy coherence. Same word, opposite-seeming
effect. Why?

The resolution is that they are the *same physical process* seen at two scales. In both
cases a system $S$ entangles with a partner $E$:

$$|\psi\rangle_{SE} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}\left(|0\rangle_S|e_0\rangle_E + |1\rangle_S|e_1\rangle_E\right)$$

Coherence between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ is never actually destroyed in either case.
It *leaves the system* and becomes a property of the joint $S{+}E$ state --- the
off-diagonal terms of the full density matrix $|\psi\rangle_{SE}\langle\psi|$ are still
there. What changes is whether you can reach $E$.

### Clean two-qubit pair: you kept the receipt

$E$ is a single controllable partner qubit; you hold both halves. The coherence is still
observable because you can measure $S$ and $E$ *jointly* --- that is exactly what a
Bell/CHSH test does. The reduced state of $S$ alone,

$$\rho_S = \mathrm{Tr}_E\,|\psi\rangle_{SE}\langle\psi| = \tfrac{1}{2}I,$$

looks fully decohered (maximally mixed), but that is only because tracing out $E$ throws
away half the information. Nothing is lost; it is stored in the correlations, and it is
recoverable.

### Schrödinger's cat / measurement: coherence irretrievably dispersed

$E$ is now the environment --- the detector, the air, eventually the observer, of order
$10^{23}$ degrees of freedom. The coherence flows into correlations spread across
astronomically many particles you cannot track or reverse. To recover it you would have
to jointly measure $S$ together with *every* environmental particle in a precise entangled
basis --- practically impossible. So for all purposes the superposition is gone. That is
decoherence.

### The punchline

The reduced density matrix $\rho_S$ is *identical* in the two cases: mixed. What differs
is **reversibility and access to the purifying partner**. A clean two-qubit pair is
coherence you kept the receipt for; the cat is coherence you irretrievably dispersed.
Decoherence is just entanglement with an environment too large and uncontrolled to undo.

There is a unifying statement underneath: the loss of *local* coherence is exactly the
growth of entanglement with the partner. Monogamy of entanglement is the same coin --- the
more strongly $S$ entangles with $E$, the less coherently $S$ can behave on its own. So
"measurement," "decoherence," and "two-qubit entanglement" are one mechanism; the labels
track only how big $E$ is and whether you can get at it.


## What actually "waves" in the double slit: photons vs massive particles

In the DSE we model a path-length difference as a relative phase (the P-gate angle, a
longitude on the Bloch sphere). Behind that sits a particle propagating as two interfering
waves. A natural question: what is the wave, mathematically? For a photon people write down
oscillating $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ fields --- do we do the same for a proton, or a neutron
that has no charge? What oscillates as a neutron moves through space?

### The photon is the special case

For a photon the "wave" is a genuine physical field: the electric and magnetic vectors
$\vec E(\vec r, t)$ and $\vec B(\vec r, t)$. These are locally measurable quantities, and a
classical electromagnetic wave exists even without quantum mechanics. The photon is the
quantized excitation of that pre-existing field. So the photon is the one case where there
is a classical field to point at --- precisely because it is a massless, force-carrying
gauge field.

### For any massive particle, what propagates is the wavefunction

For a proton, neutron, or electron there is no analogous classical field. What propagates
and interferes is the complex-valued quantum wavefunction (probability amplitude):

$$\psi(\vec r, t) = A\, e^{i(\vec k \cdot \vec r - \omega t)}$$

obeying the Schrödinger equation (or the Dirac equation relativistically). This is the
honest answer to "what oscillates": the complex amplitude $\psi$ oscillates --- its real
and imaginary parts cycle as the phase $e^{i\theta}$ winds. Charge is irrelevant. A neutron
being neutral changes nothing: it still has a de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = h/p$ and
diffracts through crystals exactly like X-rays. Neutron interferometry is a standard
laboratory technique for exactly this reason.

### The crucial difference: $\psi$ is not a locally measurable field

$\vec E$ is directly observable at a point. $\psi$ is not. Only $|\psi|^2$ (a probability
density) and the relative phases between paths (via interference) are observable. The
wavefunction itself is complex and carries an unobservable overall phase. So "what
oscillates as a neutron moves" is a probability amplitude, not a substance and not a force
field. Nothing material is waving --- the wave lives in the mathematics of
likelihood-with-phase.

### This is exactly what the Bloch-sphere / H-P-H model encodes

The two slit paths have different lengths, so the amplitude accumulates a different phase
along each:

$$\psi \sim e^{i k L_1} + e^{i k L_2}$$

The observable intensity is

$$|\psi|^2 = \left| e^{i k L_1} + e^{i k L_2} \right|^2 = 2 + 2\cos\!\left(k\,\Delta L\right),$$

the fringe pattern, depending only on the path difference $\Delta L = L_2 - L_1$. The
circuit encodes precisely this: the P-gate angle $\varphi = k\,\Delta L$ is the relative
phase between the two paths, and the H-P-H sandwich converts that phase difference into a
measurable probability $\cos^2(\varphi/2)$. The two interfering "waves" are the two terms
of $\psi$; the Bloch longitude is their relative phase.

### Summary

- **Photon:** real $\vec E$, $\vec B$ fields --- special, because it is a massless gauge
  field with a genuine classical limit.
- **Proton / electron:** complex wavefunction $\psi$; Schrödinger or Dirac. Charge plays no
  role in the interference itself.
- **Neutron:** identical --- complex $\psi$, no charge required. Neutron interferometry is
  routine.
- **What oscillates:** the complex probability amplitude $\psi$ (its phase $e^{i\theta}$),
  not any physical field or substance. Only $|\psi|^2$ and relative phase are observable.

One caveat so the tidy picture does not mislead: a photon does not actually have a
position-space wavefunction $\psi(\vec r, t)$ in the clean way a massive particle does ---
being massless and relativistic, its "wavefunction" is really the electromagnetic field
itself. The statement "everything has a $\psi$" is a non-relativistic, massive-particle
statement. For the DSE that is exactly the right framework, since the interfering object is
a massive particle whose amplitude carries a path-dependent phase.
