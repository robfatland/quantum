# Q3: Operator / Basis Duality and Reinforcement


This document delves into operators and basis (top half) and then reviews the entire
conceptual frame so far (bottom half). 


Q2 developed the single-qubit framework: states on the Bloch sphere, gates as rotations, 
the Hadamard as DFT, and measurement as projection. Here in Q3 we zoom out to see the 
structural pattern that connects all of this: the operator/basis duality. The same state 
can be described in different bases, and the Fourier transform (or the Hadamard) is the 
bridge between conjugate descriptions. This pattern scales from a single qubit to the full 
continuous theory of position and momentum.


## One state, many representations


A quantum state |ψ⟩ is a single vector in Hilbert space. But we can *express* that vector 
in different bases, much as a geometric arrow in 3D can be written in Cartesian or polar 
coordinates without changing the arrow itself.

For a particle:

- **Position basis:** expand |ψ⟩ in eigenstates of the position operator → you get ψ(x), 
  the wave function in position space. |ψ(x)|² gives the probability density of finding 
  the particle at position x.

- **Momentum basis:** expand the same |ψ⟩ in eigenstates of the momentum operator → you get 
  φ(p), the wave function in momentum space. |φ(p)|² gives the probability density of 
  the particle having momentum p.

These are not two different states. They are two representations of the same vector, written 
in two different bases.


## The Fourier transform as change of basis


The connection between position and momentum representations is:

$$\phi(p) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(x) \, e^{-ipx/\hbar} \, dx$$

$$\psi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(p) \, e^{ipx/\hbar} \, dp$$

The Fourier transform *is* the change-of-basis operation — it maps components in one 
eigenbasis to components in the conjugate eigenbasis. In finite dimensions this would be 
a unitary matrix; in infinite dimensions it's an integral transform, but the role is the same.


## Conjugate observables and non-commutativity


Position and momentum are **conjugate observables**, linked by the canonical commutation 
relation:

$$[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = \hat{x}\hat{p} - \hat{p}\hat{x} = i\hbar$$

This non-commutativity is the origin of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. If ψ(x) is 
tightly peaked (position well-known), then its Fourier transform φ(p) must be spread out 
(momentum poorly known), and vice versa. This is a mathematical theorem about Fourier 
transform pairs — the physics is in the assertion that nature uses this structure.


## Operators in each basis


Each observable takes a simple form in its own eigenbasis and a complicated form in the 
conjugate basis:

| | In position basis | In momentum basis |
|---|---|---|
| Position $\hat{x}$ | Multiply by x: $\hat{x}\psi(x) = x\psi(x)$ | Derivative: $\hat{x}\phi(p) = i\hbar \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial p}$ |
| Momentum $\hat{p}$ | Derivative: $\hat{p}\psi(x) = -i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$ | Multiply by p: $\hat{p}\phi(p) = p\phi(p)$ |

This is the **operator / basis duality**: in its own eigenbasis, an operator is diagonal 
(just multiplication by the eigenvalue). In the conjugate basis, it becomes a differential 
operator. The Fourier transform swaps which is which.


## The qubit analog


For a single qubit the same structure appears in the smallest possible Hilbert space (ℂ²):

| Continuous system | Qubit system |
|---|---|
| Position basis {|x⟩} | Z-basis {|0⟩, |1⟩} |
| Momentum basis {|p⟩} | X-basis {|+⟩, |−⟩} |
| Fourier transform | Hadamard gate |
| $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$ | $\sigma_z$ and $\sigma_x$ do not commute |

The Hadamard gate is the 2-point discrete Fourier transform (up to normalization):

$$H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

It maps Z-eigenstates to X-eigenstates and back:

$$H|0\rangle = |+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$$
$$H|1\rangle = |-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle)$$
$$H|+\rangle = |0\rangle, \quad H|-\rangle = |1\rangle$$

Applied twice: $H^2 = I$. Just as the Fourier transform applied twice (with appropriate 
conventions) returns you to the original, the Hadamard is its own inverse.


> The Hadamard gate is its own inverse (without imposing conjugation) 
because it is operating on bases of just two elements.
The first kernel is the DC mean ($\times \; 1$) and the second kernel 
has a phase of $\pi$ (or $-\pi$) ($\times \; 1, \; \times \; -1$) 
so there is nothing to conjugate. Add a second qubit and now with
a four-element basis we no longer call it a Hadamard gate; rather it
is a Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) and we *do* need to conjugate
to go from operator to inverse. 


## Complementarity in the qubit


If a qubit is in state |0⟩, it has a definite Z-value. Apply H and it becomes |+⟩ — now 
it has a definite X-value but a completely uncertain Z-value. This is the discrete analog 
of the uncertainty principle: certainty in one basis implies maximal uncertainty in the 
conjugate basis.

In quantum algorithms:
- **H at the start** creates superposition (explores all computational inputs simultaneously)
- **H at the end** causes interference (amplitudes combine constructively for correct answers, destructively for wrong ones)

This is the computational use of the operator / basis duality: you set up a problem in 
one basis, let quantum dynamics evolve it, and read out the answer in the conjugate basis.


## The Pauli matrices and the Bloch sphere


The three Pauli matrices define the three complementary measurement axes for a single qubit:

$$\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad 
\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad 
\sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Any single-qubit state can be written as a point on the **Bloch sphere**, parameterized by 
two angles θ and φ:

$$|\psi\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle$$

The north pole (θ = 0) is |0⟩, the south pole (θ = π) is |1⟩, the equator contains all 
equal superpositions (including |+⟩ and |−⟩). The Pauli matrices generate rotations about 
the x, y, and z axes of this sphere.

The Hadamard gate corresponds to a 180° rotation about an axis halfway between x and z — 
which is geometrically why it swaps the Z-basis and X-basis.


## Summary of the duality

The central idea: a quantum system has complementary descriptions connected by a unitary 
transformation (Fourier transform in infinite dimensions, Hadamard in the qubit case). 
Each description makes one observable simple (diagonal) and the conjugate observable 
complicated (off-diagonal or differential). Non-commutativity of conjugate operators is 
not a defect but the structural feature that enables superposition, interference, and 
ultimately quantum computational advantage.

### Why is the relative phase $\phi$ physically meaningful?

This was identified as a gap during the knowledge check. The key question: if Z-basis 
measurement only depends on $\theta$ (i.e. $P(|0\rangle) = \cos^2(\theta/2)$, 
independent of $\phi$), then how is $\phi$ observable at all?

The answer: **measurement in a different basis reveals $\phi$.**

The hardware always measures in the Z-basis — it projects onto $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$ 
and reports a classical bit. But you can *effectively* measure in any basis by applying a 
gate before the Z-measurement:

| To measure in... | Apply this gate first | Then measure Z | Outcome 0 means... | Outcome 1 means... |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Z-basis | (nothing) | as-is | state was $\|0\rangle$ | state was $\|1\rangle$ |
| X-basis | H | then Z | state was $\|+\rangle$ | state was $\|-\rangle$ |
| Y-basis | $S^\dagger H$ | then Z | state was $\|{+i}\rangle$ | state was $\|{-i}\rangle$ |

**Concrete example:** Consider the equatorial state 
$|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}|1\rangle)$. This has 
$\theta = \pi/2$ (equator), so Z-measurement always gives 50/50 regardless of $\phi$. 
But if you apply H first:

$$H|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{2}[(1 + e^{i\phi})|0\rangle + (1 - e^{i\phi})|1\rangle]$$

Now the probabilities are:
- $P(|0\rangle) = |1 + e^{i\phi}|^2 / 4 = \cos^2(\phi/2)$
- $P(|1\rangle) = |1 - e^{i\phi}|^2 / 4 = \sin^2(\phi/2)$

The relative phase $\phi$ is now encoded in the measurement statistics. It was always 
physically real; you just had to ask the right question (measure in the right basis) to see it.

**The general principle:** "Measuring in basis B" means applying the unitary that maps 
B-eigenstates to Z-eigenstates, then doing the standard Z-measurement. The hardware 
never changes; you change what you present to it.




### The dual role of operators: gates versus observables

The Pauli matrices are both **unitary** and **Hermitian**. This means they serve two 
distinct roles:

**As gates (unitary role):** $\sigma_x|0\rangle = |1\rangle$. The operator transforms 
the state — a 180° rotation about the x-axis. This is deterministic and reversible.

**As observables (Hermitian role):** "Measure $\sigma_z$" means: project the state onto 
the eigenstates of $\sigma_z$ (which are $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$) and obtain one of 
the eigenvalues (+1 or −1). This is probabilistic and irreversible.

The eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix are always real — which is why measurement outcomes 
are real numbers. The eigenvectors of a Hermitian matrix are orthogonal — which is why 
measurement outcomes are mutually exclusive.

What does it mean to "measure $\sigma_x$"? It means: find the eigenvalues of $\sigma_x$ 
(+1 and −1) and the eigenstates ($|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$). The measurement produces 
+1 (collapsing to $|+\rangle$) or −1 (collapsing to $|-\rangle$), with probabilities 
determined by the state's projection onto these eigenstates.

Operationally: measuring $\sigma_x$ is the same as applying H then measuring $\sigma_z$. 
The hardware only implements Z-measurement; everything else is achieved by pre-rotation.


### Measurement: what makes it different

Gates are unitary: they preserve the full state (two continuous degrees of freedom). 
They are deterministic and reversible.

Measurement is a fundamentally different operation:
- It is **probabilistic**: you cannot predict the outcome for a single shot.
- It is **irreversible**: the state collapses to an eigenstate; the prior superposition 
  is lost.
- It is **basis-dependent**: what you learn depends on which observable you measure.
- It **extracts classical information**: the output is a bit (0 or 1), not a quantum state.

The reason we repeat circuits many times (shots) is that a single measurement yields 
one random sample from the probability distribution. The distribution itself — which 
encodes the answer to the computation — can only be reconstructed from many samples. 
This is inherently statistical.

To reconstruct the *full* state (both $\theta$ and $\phi$), you need to measure in 
*multiple* bases across many shots. This is called **quantum state tomography**:
- Z-measurement statistics give $\theta$ (the probability balance)
- X-measurement statistics give $\phi$ (the relative phase)
- Y-measurement provides a consistency check

No single measurement basis reveals everything. Complementary information lives in 
complementary bases — this is the uncertainty principle made operational.


### The Hadamard as DFT: what does this analogy actually mean?

The 2-point DFT of a vector $(x_0, x_1)$ produces two coefficients:
- $X_0 = (x_0 + x_1)/\sqrt{2}$ — the "DC" or average (how much are the two values alike?)
- $X_1 = (x_0 - x_1)/\sqrt{2}$ — the "oscillation" (how much do they differ?)

For a qubit state $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$, applying H produces:
- New amplitude for $|0\rangle$: $(\alpha + \beta)/\sqrt{2}$ — "are $\alpha$ and $\beta$ similar?"
- New amplitude for $|1\rangle$: $(\alpha - \beta)/\sqrt{2}$ — "do they differ?"

The Z-basis asks "which position?" The X-basis asks "what frequency?" (i.e. "same or 
different?"). Transforming between these two questions is exactly what the Fourier transform 
does, and the Hadamard is the smallest version of it.

In quantum algorithms this is powerful because:
- You start in the Z-basis (definite position: $|0\rangle$)
- Apply H to enter the X-basis (superposition: $|+\rangle$)
- Perform computation (which encodes information in relative phases)
- Apply H again to convert phase information back into Z-basis amplitudes
- Measure in Z to read out the answer

The information flows from "position" to "frequency" and back, with the computation 
happening in "frequency space" where parallelism lives.


### What the knowledge check revealed

The primary gaps identified were:

1. **Measurement in other bases** — the mechanism by which $\phi$ becomes observable 
   (apply a rotation, then measure Z). This is now addressed above.

2. **The postulate that demands $\mathbb{C}$** — not a single sentence to memorize but 
   an empirical fact: single particles exhibit self-interference (double slit), which 
   requires continuous phase. Complex superposition is the minimal mathematical structure 
   that supports this.

3. **The DFT analogy** — not nonsensical but initially hard to articulate. The key is 
   that "time domain" = Z-basis = "which slot has the amplitude?" and "frequency domain" 
   = X-basis = "are the amplitudes in phase or out of phase?"

4. **What Pauli matrices measure** — their eigenvalues (±1) are the measurement outcomes; 
   their eigenvectors define the collapse targets. "Measure $\sigma_z$" means "collapse to 
   $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$"; "measure $\sigma_x$" means "collapse to $|+\rangle$ or 
   $|-\rangle$."

5. **Why measurement is irreversible** — gates preserve the full state; measurement 
   discards one degree of freedom permanently (within the standard formalism). The 
   connection to information conservation and black holes is genuine but requires more 
   machinery than we have built here.

These are not trivial gaps — they represent the conceptual joints where the formalism 
bends. Understanding measurement as basis-dependent projection, and understanding that 
complementary bases reveal complementary information, is the single most important 
structural insight for what comes next (multi-qubit systems, entanglement, algorithms).
